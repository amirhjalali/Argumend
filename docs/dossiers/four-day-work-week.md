# Evidence Dossier & Adversarial Audit: The Four-Day Work Week (`four-day-work-week`)

- **Audit date:** 2026-11-23
- **Playbook:** `docs/TOPIC_ANALYSIS_PLAYBOOK.md`
- **Root question:** Should a four-day week with no pay cut be adopted broadly?
- **Constituencies:**
  1. **Reduced-hours advocates** (4 Day Week Global, Autonomy, labor unions, wellbeing researchers) arguing that cutting hours at full pay (`100:80:100`) trims meeting slack, cuts burnout, and maintains output.
  2. **Empirical labor economists & skeptical employers** (CIPD, labor-market economists studying statutory hour cuts) arguing that volunteer pilots suffer severe self-selection and that cutting hours by 20% at constant pay raises unit labor costs wherever output is tied to coverage hours.
  3. **Frontline & equity skeptics** warning that voluntary four-day weeks concentrate in salaried office jobs, leaving the majority of hourly/shift workers behind—while statutory mandates risk turnover, work intensification, or dual-job holding.
- **Perception-gap hypothesis:** Public debate often sounds like a fight over whether workers enjoy a three-day weekend. Almost nobody disputes that cutting hours with full pay feels better than working five days—or that cramming 40 hours into four 10-hour shifts is a different, harsher trade-off. The real fight turns on three harder questions: whether pilot productivity survives randomization outside self-selected office firms, whether wellbeing gains last past the first year without intensifying the workday, and how much of a 20% hour cut productivity can absorb across an economy where more than half of workers are paid by the hour.

## Stage 2 — Verified Evidence Dossier

### D1 — Iceland public-sector trials (2015–2019)
- **Citation:** Gudmundur D. Haraldsson & Jack Kellam (June 2021), *Going Public: Iceland's Journey to a Shorter Working Week*, Autonomy & Alda.
- **URL:** `https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ICELAND_4DW.pdf` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** institutional / advocacy research (joint trade-union think tank & UK think tank)
- **Interest disclosure:** Co-authored by Alda (Icelandic association advocating shorter hours) and Autonomy (pro-shorter-hours think tank), analyzing government-run municipal and national trials.
- **Verified finding:** Two trials (2015–2019) cut hours from 40 to 35–36 hours/week at unchanged pay for over 2,500 public-sector workers (~1% of Iceland's workforce), including offices, hospitals, police, and social services. Service provision and productivity were maintained or improved across nearly all workplaces. By June 2021, 86% of Iceland's working population had moved to shorter hours or gained the contractual right to shorten them.
- **Scope limit:** Not a randomized trial; cut was 4–5 hours/week (to 35–36 hours, not 32 hours); conducted in a small, highly unionized, high-trust public sector.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for`

### D2 — UK four-day week pilot & one-year follow-up (2022–2024)
- **Citation:** Autonomy, University of Cambridge & Boston College (Feb 2024), *Making It Stick: The UK Four-Day Week Pilot One Year On* (follow-up to Feb 2023 baseline report).
- **URL:** `https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/making-it-stick_-1.pdf` (verified 2026-11-23); baseline: `https://autonomy.work/wp-content/uploads/2023/02/The-results-are-in-The-UKs-four-day-week-pilot.pdf`
- **Source kind:** institutional / academic collaboration
- **Interest disclosure:** Pilot coordinated by 4 Day Week Global and Autonomy (advocacy organizations), evaluated with university researchers (Cambridge, Oxford, Boston College).
- **Verified finding:** 61 UK organizations (~2,900 employees) ran a 6-month trial (June–Dec 2022) under the 100:80:100 model, reporting an average weighted revenue rise of 1.4% and 56 of 61 firms continuing afterward. One year later (Feb 2024), at least 54 of the 61 companies (89%) were still operating a four-day week, and at least 31 (51%) had made it permanent. Participating managers reported sustained work-culture changes (fewer meetings, clearer prioritization).
- **Scope limit:** Volunteer sample with no randomized control group; heavily skewed toward small-to-midsize flexible office and service firms; financial comparisons rely on company self-reports without matched non-adopting controls.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for`

### D3 — Germany-wide four-day week pilot (2024)
- **Citation:** Prof. Dr. Julia Backmann & Dr. Felix Hoch, University of Münster (Oct 2024), *4-Day Week Germany-Wide Pilot Study* (with Intraprenör & 4 Day Week Global).
- **URL:** `https://www.wiwi.uni-muenster.de/tow/en/research/4-day-week-germany-wide-pilot-study` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** academic evaluation of advocacy-organized pilot
- **Interest disclosure:** Independently evaluated by University of Münster researchers; pilot organized by Intraprenör and 4 Day Week Global.
- **Verified finding:** 45 organizations in Germany ran a 6-month trial in 2024 combining surveys with smartwatch biometric tracking. Turnover and profit remained stable compared with prior periods; employees reported higher life satisfaction, lower stress/burnout, improved sleep and physical activity, and a drop in unmet desire for family time (64% to 50%). However, roughly 20% of participating firms opted to return to traditional schedules afterward, and a small number exited early for economic reasons.
- **Scope limit:** 6-month duration, self-selected volunteer firms, small sample (45 organizations). Both the stability among continuers and the ~20% reversion rate matter.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for` (with explicit non-universal reversion caveat)

### D4 — CIPD UK employer-perspectives survey (2022)
- **Citation:** Chartered Institute of Personnel and Development (2022), *The four-day week: Employer perspectives*.
- **URL:** `https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/reports/four-day-week/` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** institutional (UK professional body for HR)
- **Interest disclosure:** Professional HR body representing employer-side practitioners; independent of four-day-week advocacy groups.
- **Verified finding:** UK employers were broadly ambivalent about reducing working hours and saw broad adoption of a four-day week as unlikely. Employers who had tried shorter hours noted it did not fit every role inside the same organization, flagging customer-coverage gaps, coordination friction, and managing atypical or non-salaried workers.
- **Scope limit:** Qualitative and attitudinal employer perspectives rather than an experimental test of productivity.
- **Direction (`side`):** `against`

### D5 — Six-country four-day workweek study in *Nature Human Behaviour* (2025)
- **Citation:** Wen Fan, Juliet B. Schor, Orla Kelly & Guolin Gu (July 2025; print Oct 2025), "Work time reduction via a 4-day workweek finds improvements in workers' well-being," *Nature Human Behaviour* 9, 2153–2168.
- **URL / DOI:** `https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02259-6` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** peer-reviewed
- **Interest disclosure:** Academic team at Boston College and University College Dublin evaluating trials organized with 4 Day Week Global.
- **Verified finding:** Analyzed pre- and post-trial data from 2,896 employees across 141 organizations in six countries (Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, UK, US) over 6 months, alongside 12 control organizations remaining on 5-day schedules. Burnout dropped from 2.83 to 2.38 (5-point scale), job satisfaction rose from 7.07 to 7.59 (10-point scale), and mental and physical health scores improved—patterns not seen in the 12 control firms. Gains were mediated by reduced fatigue, fewer sleep problems, and higher self-reported work ability, with larger hour cuts yielding larger gains.
- **Scope limit:** 6-month trial window; organizations self-selected into the intervention rather than being randomly assigned; small non-randomized control group (12 firms); relies on self-reported wellbeing measures.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for`

### D6 — Systematic review of compressed working weeks (2008) & Belgium's legal uptake (2022–2024)
- **Citation:** Clare Bambra, Margaret Whitehead, Amanda Sowden, Jane Akers & Mark Petticrew (2008), "A hard day's night? The effects of Compressed Working Week interventions on the health and work-life balance of shift workers: a systematic review," *Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health* 62(9).
- **URL / DOI:** `https://doi.org/10.1136/jech.2007.067249` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** peer-reviewed systematic review
- **Interest disclosure:** None identified.
- **Verified finding:** Across 40 observational studies of shift workers compressing the same weekly hours into fewer, longer days (e.g., 4×10), compressed schedules "did not always improve the health of shift workers," though work-life balance generally improved and the five controlled prospective studies found no health harm; overall study quality was "not very high." Separately, after Belgium legislated a right to request a compressed four-day week (same 38–40 hours in four longer days) in November 2022, HR payroll provider Acerta reported uptake of only ~0.8% of full-time private-sector workers—illustrating how sharply workers distinguish true hour reduction from hour compression.
- **Scope limit:** Bears directly on *compressed* schedules (4×10), not reduced-hours 4×8 models; distinguishes the two mechanisms rather than disproving reduced hours.
- **Direction (`side`):** `against`

### D7 — European Social Survey working-hours and life-satisfaction study (2022)
- **Citation:** Qinglong Shao (2022), "Does less working time improve life satisfaction? Evidence from European Social Survey," *Health Economics Review*.
- **URL / DOI:** `https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-022-00396-6` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** peer-reviewed
- **Interest disclosure:** None identified.
- **Verified finding:** Analyzing Wave 10 of the European Social Survey (18,060 respondents across Europe), fewer working hours were significantly associated with higher life satisfaction even after controlling for income, with health acting as a key mediating channel.
- **Scope limit:** Observational cross-sectional survey; self-selection into shorter hours limits causal claims.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for`

### D8 — France's 35-hour workweek: OECD productivity & Askenazy's subsidy/intensity record (2000–2023)
- **Citation:** OECD Labour Productivity Statistics (`https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/gdp-per-hour-worked.html`); Philippe Askenazy (2013), "Working time regulation in France from 1996 to 2012," *Cambridge Journal of Economics* 37(2), 323–347.
- **URL / DOI:** `https://doi.org/10.1093/cje/bes084` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** official statistics + peer-reviewed economics
- **Interest disclosure:** None identified.
- **Verified finding:** After legislating a 35-hour statutory week in 2000, France maintained high GDP per hour worked—above the UK and euro-area average and only modestly below the US in OECD data. However, Askenazy (2013) documents that keeping employer costs manageable relied on large recurring social-security payroll subsidies (under the Robien and Aubry laws) and firm-level scheduling flexibility that frequently increased **work intensity** for employees producing the same output in fewer hours. Later reforms also widened overtime exemptions, leaving actual average hours above 35.
- **Scope limit:** France's mandate cut statutory hours from 39 to 35 (~10%), half the size of a 40-to-32-hour cut, and was buffered by state subsidies.
- **Direction (`side`):** `for` (compatibility with high hourly productivity, qualified by subsidy and work-intensity trade-offs)

### D9 — Quasi-experimental evaluation of France's 35-hour mandate (Estevão & Sá, 2008)
- **Citation:** Marcello Estevão & Filipa Sá (2008), "The 35-hour workweek in France: Straightjacket or welfare improvement?" *Economic Policy* 23(55), 417–463.
- **URL / DOI:** `https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0327.2008.00204.x` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** peer-reviewed economics
- **Interest disclosure:** None identified (IMF / MIT affiliated authors).
- **Verified finding:** Exploiting France's staggered rollout (large firms mandated in 2000, small firms in 2002), Estevão and Sá found aggregate employment was largely unaffected, while relative hourly wages rose in regulated large firms (because monthly pay was protected as hours fell), labor turnover increased as firms shed higher-cost workers, some workers migrated to unregulated small firms, dual-job holding rose, and managers reported negative welfare effects—while women benefited from better schedule coordination.
- **Scope limit:** Measures a mandatory, top-down 39-to-35-hour law in France's specific labor market; does not test voluntary firm-level 32-hour adoption.
- **Direction (`side`):** `against`

### D10 — Scale of hourly-paid work in the United States (BLS, 2024)
- **Citation:** U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2025), *Characteristics of Minimum Wage Workers, 2024* (Report 1115).
- **URL:** `https://www.bls.gov/opub/reports/minimum-wage/2024/` (verified 2026-11-23)
- **Source kind:** government statistics
- **Interest disclosure:** Official U.S. federal statistical agency.
- **Verified finding:** In 2024, **80.3 million** U.S. workers aged 16 and older were paid at hourly rates, representing **55.6%** of all wage and salary workers.
- **Scope limit:** Counts hourly-paid workers rather than directly measuring task compressability; some hourly roles do have slack, and some salaried roles (e.g., medical residents) are coverage-bound. Still establishes the baseline scale of hours-tied employment that voluntary white-collar pilots barely sample.
- **Direction (`side`):** `against`
