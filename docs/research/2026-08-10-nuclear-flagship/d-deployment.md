**Research Brief D: Deployment Realities**

As of August 10, 2026, the strongest pro-nuclear deployment case is not “the United States can instantly build a 1970s-scale fleet.” It is that the federal constraint set has materially changed: the ADVANCE Act was signed in July 2024, the NRC finalized 10 CFR Part 53 in March 2026, and the agency is now advertising 17- to 18-month review schedules for specific advanced-reactor construction applications rather than the older generic 30- to 36-month schedules. The strongest skeptical case is that licensing speed is only one gate: the U.S. has no active large-reactor construction program after Vogtle, thin nuclear construction labor capacity, constrained heavy manufacturing, limited HALEU fuel supply, and demand growth that is urgent before most new nuclear can plausibly arrive.

**Regulation**

The NRC’s traditional new-reactor framework is split among Part 50 construction permit plus operating license, Part 52 combined licenses and design certifications, and now Part 53 as an optional “risk-informed, performance-based, and technology-inclusive” framework for commercial nuclear plants. (https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/licensing-process-fs; https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/modernizing/rulemaking/part-53) Before the recent reforms, DOE/GAIN summarized generic NRC advanced non-light-water reactor licensing timelines as 36 months for a construction permit, 36 months for an operating license, 30 months for a combined license referencing a certified design, and 36 months for a combined license not referencing a certified design. (https://gain.inl.gov/industry-support/regulatory-support/frequently-asked-questions-about-regulatory-support/)

The ADVANCE Act of 2024 required NRC actions on efficient and predictable reviews, expedited procedures for qualifying new-reactor applications, advanced fuel, brownfield and former fossil-site reviews, microreactor strategies, fusion, NRC workforce, and reduced fees for advanced reactor applicants, while maintaining the NRC’s safety and security mission. (https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/governing-laws/advance-act/about-advance-act) The NRC says it has already implemented revised NEIMA milestone schedules aligned with 12- and 18-month periods, set a 17-month review for TVA Clinch River, completed TerraPower’s Kemmerer construction-permit safety review in 18 months, set an 18-month review for Dow/X-energy’s Long Mott application, and reduced the advanced-reactor applicant hourly rate from $318 to $148 effective October 1, 2025. (https://www.nrc.gov/about-nrc/governing-laws/advance-act/licensing-efficiencies)

Part 53’s final rule was published in March 2026 as 91 FR 15696, adding a technology-inclusive pathway that allows risk-informed treatment of systems, structures, and components and greater use of non-nuclear codes where safety significance permits. (https://regulations.justia.com/regulations/fedreg/2026/03/30/2026-06048.html) The steelman for “regulation is the binding constraint” is that NRC review historically forced novel designs through light-water-reactor-era structures, created uncertainty before capital could be committed, and treated additional safety margin as a reason to add requirements rather than reduce them; Breakthrough’s Adam Stein argues the NRC’s problem is “decision architecture,” not lack of technical skill. (https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/nuclear-energy-innovation/the-nrcs-problem-isnt-caution-its-architecture; https://thebreakthrough.org/issues/nuclear-energy-innovation/nrc-regulations-must-be-credible-predictable-timely-and-proportionate-to-risk)

The steelman counter is that nuclear licensing is slow because the underlying activity is complex, capital-intensive, site-specific, security-sensitive, and potentially catastrophic if badly executed. UCS argues the ADVANCE Act weakens the NRC’s independent safety mission and that many non-light-water reactors have unresolved safety, security, waste, and proliferation questions rather than intrinsically lower hazards. (https://www.ucs.org/about/news/advance-act-retreat-nuclear-power-safety; https://www.ucs.org/resources/advanced-isnt-always-better) On this view, a shorter review clock is useful only if application quality, supply-chain qualification, emergency planning, cyber/security review, fuel availability, and construction oversight remain strong.

**Industrial Capacity**

The U.S. nuclear workforce is small relative to expansion ambitions. DOE reported 67,900 U.S. nuclear energy workers in 2024 across fuels and electric power generation, with 85% in electric power generation, and reported that more than 80% of employers across nuclear construction, manufacturing, wholesale trade, professional services, and utilities had at least “some” hiring difficulty in 2024. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/3-workforce-trends-nuclear-energy-2025) That supports both sides: a pro-build case says high wages and revived demand can attract labor; the skeptical case says a 67,900-worker base is not enough to support multiple simultaneous first-of-a-kind megaprojects without bottlenecks.

Heavy manufacturing is another constraint. A World Nuclear Association heavy-manufacturing summary says the U.S. lost large pressure-vessel and steam-generator manufacturing capability by the turn of the century, although firms such as BWXT and ATI retain or are rebuilding some capability, especially for SMR-scale components. (https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/nuclear-power-reactors/other/heavy-manufacturing-of-power-plants) A 2026 industry report stated that reactor pressure vessels and steam generators for large reactors can require 42- to 54-month lead times and that such forgings are made at only a few global facilities, none in the U.S. for the largest AP1000-scale items. (https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/electric-power/050726-us-doe-might-finance-long-lead-nuclear-reactor-components) That makes “order early, standardize, and aggregate demand” a serious policy lever, but also means 2030 power dates require procurement decisions in the mid-2020s.

Fuel is a harder near-term constraint for many advanced reactors. DOE says most advanced reactors require HALEU enriched between 5% and less than 20% U-235, that domestic HALEU demand could reach 50 metric tons per year by 2035, and that HALEU is not yet commercially available at scale from domestic suppliers. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/what-high-assay-low-enriched-uranium-haleu; https://www.energy.gov/ne/haleu-availability-program) DOE said in 2024 that Russia supplied about 44% of global enrichment services and 20% to 30% of enriched uranium product used in the U.S. and Europe. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/biden-harris-administration-takes-action-secure-nuclear-fuel-supply-chain-equip) Congress banned Russian low-enriched uranium imports beginning 90 days after May 13, 2024, but allowed DOE waivers through January 1, 2028, with annual caps through 2027. (https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/1042; https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A42+section%3A2297h-10a+edition%3Aprelim%29)

Centrus has made real progress but at demonstration scale: it delivered 20 kg of HALEU in 2023, was contracted for 900 kg by June 30, 2025, received a one-year extension through June 30, 2026, and announced in July 2026 that it had produced more than 1,900 kg cumulatively over the life of the contract. (https://investors.centrusenergy.com/news-releases/news-release-details/centrus-energy-secures-contract-extension-department-energy; https://www.centrusenergy.com/news/centrus-signs-contract-with-department-of-energy-for-900-million-award-intends-to-transition-haleu-production-cascade-to-commercial-operation/) DOE also reported a 1.7 metric ton HALEU transfer from Japan in 2026, which helps bridge early supply but is not a recurring commercial supply chain. (https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/articles/us-secures-largest-ever-haleu-shipment-power-american-nuclear-industry)

Construction productivity is the deepest skeptical evidence. A peer-reviewed Joule study found recent U.S. nuclear plant labor productivity was up to 13 times lower than industry expectations and that containment-building costs more than doubled from 1976 to 2017 partly because of declining on-site productivity. (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S254243512030458X) Lovering, Yip, and Nordhaus found global nuclear construction-cost experience differs by country and era, with no inherent universal cost-escalation trend, but the U.S. record is still one of severe escalation. (https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2%3A2023071) Vogtle is ambiguous: Unit 4 benefited from Unit 3 lessons during startup testing, but the two-unit expansion still took about 11 years and cost more than $30 billion against original expectations of $14 billion and 2016/2017 operations. (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php/detail.php?id=61963; https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/hot-functional-testing-complete-at-vogtle-4)

**Project Timelines**

The near-term U.S. pipeline is mostly restarts plus first-of-a-kind demonstrations. Vogtle Unit 3 entered commercial operation in July 2023 and Vogtle Unit 4 in April 2024, each about 1,114 MW, and EIA said no reactors were under construction in the U.S. after Vogtle Unit 4 began commercial operation. (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php/detail.php?id=61963)

TerraPower’s Kemmerer Unit 1 received an NRC construction permit on March 9, 2026, after a March 2024 application, and TerraPower began nuclear construction in April 2026. (https://www.nrc.gov/node/2156776; https://www.terrapower.com/TerraPower-Commences-Construction-on-Americas-First-Utility-Scale-Advanced-Nuclear-Power-Plant) The Natrium project is 345 MWe with storage-enabled boost to 500 MW, must still receive a separate operating license, and DOE says completion is expected in 2030. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nrc-issues-construction-permit-terrapowers-natrium-advanced-reactor) A TerraPower filing cited an earliest construction completion date of August 30, 2030, and latest with contingency of February 28, 2031. (https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML2534/ML25343A199.pdf)

TVA’s Clinch River BWRX-300 application was submitted in April/May 2025, NRC completed its final environmental review on April 6, 2026, issued a final safety evaluation on June 25, 2026, and targeted a construction-permit decision for fall 2026. (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/who-were-working-with/applicant-projects/clinch-river) GE Vernova said the first Clinch River BWRX-300 targets commercial operation in the early 2030s, while TVA’s CEO was reported as planning 2033 after DOE funding accelerated the schedule by two years. (https://www.gevernova.com/news/press-releases/us-department-energy-announces-400-million-funding-accelerate-deployment-nation-first-commercial-small-modular-nuclear-reactor; https://www.neimagazine.com/news/clinch-river-smr-project/)

Palisades permanently ceased operations on May 20, 2022, rescinded its cessation certifications effective August 25, 2025, and as of July 2026 still had inspections and restart work underway; ANS reported no firm restart date had been announced. (https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/pali; https://www.ans.org/news/article-8187/palisades-restart-projects-holtec-ipo-lawsuit-dismissal-but-no-restart-date/) Crane Clean Energy Center, formerly Three Mile Island Unit 1, shut down in September 2019; Constellation’s Microsoft-backed restart would add 835 MW and was originally expected online in 2028, while 2026 reporting said Constellation still sought 2027 despite possible grid delays. (https://investors.constellationenergy.com/news-releases/news-release-details/constellation-launch-crane-clean-energy-center-restoring-jobs; https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/constellation-seeks-regulators-help-for-2027-plant-restart) Duane Arnold shut down on August 10, 2020, after derecho damage, and NextEra says it anticipates full operation by Q1 2029. (https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/duan; https://www.nexteraenergy.com/duane-arnold-energy-center/faq.html)

Compared with renewables and storage, the speed difference is stark. EIA said developers added 40.4 GW of U.S. utility-scale capacity in 2023, planned 62.8 GW in 2024, including 36.4 GW solar and 14.3 GW battery storage, and later forecast 26 GW solar in 2025 plus 22 GW in 2026. (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=61424; https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=64364) These GW are not equivalent to nuclear GW because capacity factors, dispatchability, and storage duration differ, but they show that renewables and batteries can add nameplate capacity on annual cycles while new nuclear projects operate on decade-scale cycles.

**Demand Context**

Demand growth strengthens the nuclear case but does not by itself prove nuclear can arrive in time. EPRI’s 2024 study put data centers at 4.6% to 9.1% of U.S. electricity generation by 2030. (https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/epri-study-data-centers-could-consume-up-to-9-of-us-electricity-generation-by-2030-302157970.html) EPRI’s 2026 update projected U.S. data-center electricity use at 383 to 793 TWh per year by 2030, or roughly 2x to 4x 2024 levels. (https://powering-intelligence.epri.com/annual-peak-use.html) Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s 2026 update estimated data centers could account for 11.8% of total U.S. electricity by 2030, with scenarios from 9.5% to 15.3%. (https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/united-states-data-center-energy-2025) Grid Strategies reported utility forecasts showing roughly 166 GW of peak-demand growth by 2030, about 20% above 2025 peak load, with data centers accounting for roughly 55% of projected demand growth. (https://gridstrategiesllc.com/forecasting-for-large-loads/)

The pro-nuclear inference is qualitative: large new loads need 24/7 firm, high-capacity-factor, low-carbon electricity, and data-center buyers have already signed nuclear-linked PPAs such as Microsoft-Crane and Meta-Clinton. (https://investors.constellationenergy.com/news-releases/news-release-details/constellation-launch-crane-clean-energy-center-restoring-jobs; https://www.constellationenergy.com/news/2025/constellation-meta-sign-20-year-deal-for-clean-reliable-nuclear-energy-in-illinois.html) The skeptical inference is timing: if most incremental load arrives before 2030, then Palisades, Crane, Duane Arnold, uprates, gas, renewables, batteries, transmission, demand response, and load-management rules carry the near-term burden, while new reactors mostly affect the 2030s and 2040s.

**Politics, Siting, International Benchmarks**

Public opinion is more favorable than it was. Gallup found 61% of U.S. adults favored nuclear energy in 2025, one point below its trend high, with 74% of Republicans, 64% of independents, and 46% of Democrats in favor. (https://news.gallup.com/poll/659180/nuclear-energy-support-near-record-high.aspx) Pew found about 59% favored more nuclear power plants in 2025, up from 43% in 2020, and Pew’s 2026 survey found 54% of Republicans and 38% of Democrats wanted the federal government to encourage nuclear production. (https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/10/16/support-for-expanding-nuclear-power-is-up-in-both-parties-since-2020/; https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2026/04/03/americans-shifting-views-on-energy-issues/)

State restrictions are loosening but not gone. DOE says Wisconsin repealed its moratorium in 2016, Kentucky in 2017, Montana in 2021, West Virginia in 2022, Illinois fully removed its size limitation in January 2026, and New Jersey repealed its moratorium in April 2026. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/what-nuclear-moratorium) DOE’s same page contains an apparent inconsistency by also listing New Jersey among states with statewide moratoriums; New Jersey’s current legal status is therefore **UNVERIFIED** from that page alone. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/what-nuclear-moratorium) DOE lists California, Hawaii, Maine, Massachusetts, Minnesota, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Vermont as still having statewide moratoriums or restrictions. (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/what-nuclear-moratorium) Consent-based siting remains important because NRC licensing does not eliminate state, local, tribal, environmental, water, transmission, and emergency-planning politics.

Internationally, China is the main pro-scale benchmark. EIA reported in June 2026 that China added 24 GW of nuclear capacity from 2016 to 2024, added 1.1 GW in 2025 and 2.2 GW through May 2026, and had 36 reactors under construction, more than 49% of global nuclear construction. (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67746) IEA said reactors totaling 78 GW were under construction globally in 15 countries, about half of that capacity in China, with China expected to reach about 100 GW by around 2030. (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear) South Korea is the smaller standardization benchmark: IEA listed Korea among advanced economies with two reactors under construction as of March 2026, and the World Nuclear Association database shows Shin-Hanul-4 started construction on May 29, 2026. (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear; https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-reactor-database)

The transfer dispute is credible. Proponents say China and South Korea show nuclear can be built at scale with standard designs, experienced EPC teams, stable regulation, and programmatic procurement. Skeptics answer that China’s state-directed financing, industrial labor model, utility structure, siting authority, and supply chain do not transfer cleanly to the U.S., and South Korea’s success depends on fleet standardization that the U.S. has not yet committed to.

**Neutral Disagreement Propositions**

1. Whether NRC reform has changed the binding constraint or merely shortened one stage of a still decade-long deployment chain. Evidence that would resolve it: actual NRC approval, construction, fuel-load, and operation dates for Kemmerer, Clinch River, Long Mott, and restart projects.

2. Whether U.S. nuclear construction productivity can recover through repeat builds after Vogtle. Evidence that would resolve it: fixed-price or audited cost and schedule data for the next 2 to 6 standardized units.

3. Whether HALEU and heavy-component supply chains can scale before project demand arrives. Evidence that would resolve it: contracted annual domestic HALEU output in metric tons, qualified deconversion/fabrication capacity, and delivered long-lead forgings.

4. Whether AI/data-center load growth is durable, coincident with system peaks, and politically acceptable at forecast scale. Evidence that would resolve it: interconnection conversion rates, realized data-center utilization, utility load-factor data, and ratepayer cost-allocation outcomes.

5. Whether demand growth changes nuclear from “valuable clean firm option” to “necessary large-scale buildout.” Evidence that would resolve it: regional capacity-expansion modeling with transmission, storage duration, gas constraints, carbon limits, reliability standards, and actual large-load siting behavior.
