**Research Report**

**Question: Can renewables plus storage provide reliability equivalent to firm nuclear generation on a decarbonizing U.S. grid?**

The strongest shared starting point is that U.S. reliability is not mainly a question of whether wind, solar, and batteries “work.” They already work at large scale. The harder question is whether a system dominated by weather-dependent generation can supply resource adequacy, operating reliability, and resilience through rare multi-day and seasonal stress events at costs and deployment rates comparable to alternatives such as nuclear, gas with CCS, geothermal, hydrogen turbines, hydropower, and demand flexibility. The crux is the difference between 80–95% clean electricity, where a broad literature finds high renewables can be integrated with transmission, storage, flexible demand, and residual firm resources, and the last 5–10%, where costs, land, transmission, and low-probability weather risk dominate the disagreement. NREL’s 2035 100% clean electricity study makes that threshold explicit: “seasonal storage becomes important” around 80–95% clean generation because of multi-day-to-seasonal renewable-demand mismatch, with modeled seasonal capacity ranging from about 100 GW to 680 GW by 2035 (https://www.nrel.gov/grid/news/program/2022/exploring-the-big-challenge-ahead-insights-on-the-path-to-a-net-zero-power-sector-by-2035).

**Steelman: Renewables Plus Storage Can Fully Substitute For New Nuclear**

The best pro-substitution argument is not that four-hour lithium-ion batteries alone replace nuclear plants. It is that a portfolio of wind, solar, short-duration batteries, long-duration storage, demand response, hydropower, geothermal, transmission, overbuild, curtailment, and clean fuels can provide equivalent reliability without requiring new nuclear construction. NREL’s Renewable Electricity Futures Study found that commercially available renewable technologies, with a more flexible grid, could supply 80% of U.S. electricity in 2050 while meeting hourly demand in every region (https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/renewable-electricity-futures-study-executive-summary/). A decade later, NREL said the original 80% finding has largely been confirmed and may have been conservative because lithium-ion costs fell from roughly $1,200/kWh around the study era to about $130/kWh in 2020 (https://www.nrel.gov/news/feature/2022/re-futures/).

The current cost and deployment trend strengthens this side. EIA reported that U.S. utility-scale battery capacity exceeded 26 GW in 2024 after 10.4 GW was added that year, and developers planned another 19.6 GW in 2025 (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=64705). EIA’s February 2026 generator inventory said developers planned 24 GW of battery additions in 2026, compared with a record 15 GW added in 2025, and that more than 40 GW of battery capacity had been added over the preceding five years (https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=67205). BloombergNEF’s 2025 battery survey put average lithium-ion pack prices at $108/kWh, stationary storage packs at $70/kWh, and LFP packs at $81/kWh (https://about.bnef.com/insights/clean-transport/lithium-ion-battery-pack-prices-fall-to-108-per-kilowatt-hour-despite-rising-metal-prices-bloombergnef/). NREL’s 2025 utility-scale battery projection gives 4-hour system costs of $152/kWh, $247/kWh, and $349/kWh in 2035 across low, mid, and high cases, showing that credible modelers still bracket a wide cost range but expect continued declines (https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/cost-projections-for-utility-scale-battery-storage-2025-update/).

Real grids support the claim that batteries materially improve reliability at high renewable shares. California grew from 500 MW of battery storage in 2018 to more than 16,900 MW by July 31, 2025, and the state’s energy agency says storage dispatches at sunset to reduce dependence on fossil generation during peak load (https://www.energy.ca.gov/data-reports/energy-almanac/california-electricity-data/california-energy-storage-system-survey). California also reported nearly 17,000 MW of battery storage in November 2025, no Flex Alerts since 2022, and battery capacity equal to roughly one-quarter of its record peak demand for several hours (https://www.energy.ca.gov/news/2025-11/californias-battery-storage-fleet-continues-record-growth-strengthening-grid). In CAISO, EIA reported that utility-scale solar capacity rose 19% to 25 GW and net battery capacity rose 79% to 16 GW from April 2024 to April 2026, while battery discharge in the first five months of 2026 tripled versus the same period in 2024 (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67784).

Texas offers a parallel case. EIA reported ERCOT had 8.1 GW of utility-scale battery capacity at the end of 2024, and the Dallas Fed reported batteries set an ERCOT record of 7.1 GW of discharge at 8 p.m. on July 30, 2025, when solar had fallen and net load peaked (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=66164; https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2025/1104-golding-grid). South Australia shows that a grid can run with very high wind and solar shares while batteries provide frequency and system-security services: ARENA says the Hornsdale expansion demonstrated large-scale battery inertia and other grid services needed as synchronous generation declines (https://arena.gov.au/projects/hornsdale-power-reserve-upgrade/).

This side also argues that “firm power needed” does not imply “new nuclear needed.” IPCC AR6 says high-renewable electricity systems can use storage, hydrogen, transmission, flexible non-renewable generation, advanced controls, and demand-side response, and that broader portfolios lower integration costs (https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/). Enhanced geothermal is moving from demonstration to commercial scale: Fervo said Cape Station Phase I is planned to deliver 100 MW in 2026 and Phase II another 400 MW by 2028 (https://fervoenergy.com/fervo-secures-new-financing-to-accelerate-development/). Demand response and flexible electrified loads are also real resources; NREL’s Electrification Futures operational study found demand-side flexibility can reduce curtailment and increase utilization of lower-cost generators in high-electrification, high-renewable systems (https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/electrification-futures-study-operational-analysis-of-us-power-sy/).

The Jacobson wind-water-solar position is the most aggressive version of this side. Jacobson et al. argued in PNAS that the continental U.S. could keep the grid reliable at low cost with 100% wind, water, and solar across all energy uses by 2050–2055 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26598655/). After criticism, Jacobson et al. reaffirmed that conclusion and rejected claims that their assumptions invalidated the result (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5495290/). A steelman version does not need every Jacobson assumption to be correct; it can say the broader evidence since 2015 has moved toward cheaper batteries, more mature inverters, faster solar deployment, and greater operational familiarity with high-renewable grids.

**Steelman: A Reliable Deep-Decarbonized Grid Needs Firm Generation At Nuclear Scale**

The strongest firm-resource argument is that the last 5–10% of decarbonization is qualitatively different from the first 80–90%. Sepulveda, Jenkins, de Sisternes, and Lester modeled nearly 1,000 deep-decarbonization cases and found that availability of firm low-carbon resources such as nuclear, gas with CCS, and bioenergy reduced costs by 10–62% in fully decarbonized cases; below 50 gCO2/kWh, firm resources lowered costs in the vast majority of cases (https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/the-role-of-firm-low-carbon-electricity-resources-in-deep-decarbo/). MIT’s “Future of Nuclear Energy in a Carbon-Constrained World” similarly frames nuclear as valuable because it is a scalable low-carbon source that can reduce the cost and difficulty of carbon-constrained electricity systems if construction cost and policy barriers are addressed (https://energy.mit.edu/research/future-nuclear-energy-carbon-constrained-world/).

This side’s central technical point is weather correlation. Batteries solve hourly ramps; they do not automatically solve continent-scale low wind and solar periods. A Nature Communications study using 39 years of hourly reanalysis data found that, with annual wind-plus-solar generation equal to annual demand and no storage, the most reliable systems met demand in 72–91% of hours across 42 countries; adding 12 hours of storage raised that to 83–94%, but still left hundreds of unmet hours annually in many cases (https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-26355-z). NREL’s resource-adequacy work found that as PV penetration rises, peak net load shifts into evening, morning, and winter periods, and snow cover can create shortfall risk in high-PV scenarios (https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/considerations-for-maintaining-resource-adequacy-of-electricity-s-2/). NREL’s extreme-weather work says the future “extreme” event may be moderately severe hot or cold weather coinciding with extended low wind and solar output, especially winter cold after frontal wind lulls (https://www.nrel.gov/samples/content-widgets/news2/detail2/program/2024/moderate-is-the-new-extreme-weathers-impact-on-growing-renewable-grid-operations).

The operating evidence is mixed rather than one-sided. California’s batteries reduce evening scarcity, but CAISO curtailed 3.4 million MWh of wind and solar in 2024, up 29% from 2023, with solar making up 93% of curtailed energy (https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=65364). CAISO’s own December 2024 renewables report recorded 22,397 MWh of wind and solar curtailment in December and a 15,931 MW three-hour maximum net-load ramp (https://www.caiso.com/documents/monthly-renewables-performance-report-december-2024.html). ERCOT’s Winter Storm Uri also cautions against simplistic claims that any single resource class is reliable by default: FERC and NERC found ERCOT ordered 20,000 MW of rolling blackouts, more than 4.5 million Texans lost power, and 75.6% of unplanned outages, derates, and failures to start were caused by freezing and fuel issues, with natural gas units representing 58% of affected units and wind 27% (https://www.ferc.gov/news-events/news/final-report-february-2021-freeze-underscores-winterization-recommendations). The lesson is not “renewables failed” or “gas failed”; it is that reliability depends on weatherization, fuel assurance, planning reserves, transmission, and market design.

Capacity factor also matters, though it is not the same as capacity credit. EIA’s 2024 data show nuclear at a 92.3% capacity factor, wind at 34.3%, and solar PV at 23.4% (https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?kref=QOWNqTgorKXc&kuid=d81d0575-d3e0-433a-958e-1a10487f3ed4-1736654157&t=epmt_6_07_b). A renewables-plus-storage system can reproduce firm output statistically, but doing so requires much more nameplate capacity, surplus generation, transmission, storage energy capacity, or backup fuel than the nuclear nameplate comparison suggests.

Long-duration storage remains the most important gap. DOE defines short duration as 0–10 hours, inter-day LDES as 10–36 hours, multi-day LDES as 36–160 hours, and seasonal shifting as 160+ hours, often via chemical storage such as hydrogen (https://www.energy.gov/edf/energy-storage-projects). Actually operating large long-duration assets are limited: U.S. pumped hydropower is about 22.2 GW of nameplate capacity in 2024, while NREL describes legacy pumped storage as mostly 8–12 hours (https://www.eia.gov/electricity/annual/table.php?t=epa_04_03.html; https://www.nrel.gov/grid/news/program/2021/nrel-launches-storage-futures-study-with-visionary-framework-for-dramatic-increase-in-deployment). The McIntosh CAES plant in Alabama is 110 MW and can discharge for 26 hours, but it uses natural gas heat and is one of only a small number of commercial CAES plants globally (https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/compressed-air-energy-storage). Hydrostor’s Goderich advanced CAES reference project is only 1.75 MW and more than 10 MWh, in service since 2019 (https://hydrostor.ca/project/the-goderich-a-caes-facility/). Form Energy says its iron-air battery can discharge for up to 100 hours, and a California-funded prototype completed testing with EPRI validation of more than 100 hours, but announced grid projects such as a 10 MW/1,000 MWh Ireland deployment are planned for 2029 rather than operating today (https://formenergy.com/technology/battery-technology/; https://www.energizeinnovation.fund/projects/demonstrating-aqueous-air-breathing-energy-storage-system-multi-day-resiliency; https://formenergy.com/form-energy-and-futurenergy-ireland-announce-agreement-to-deploy-first-iron-air-battery-storage-project-in-ireland/). Thermal LDES is recognized in DOE’s taxonomy for multi-day storage, but I found no operating U.S. nuclear-scale thermal-to-electric seasonal storage plant in the sources reviewed; **UNVERIFIED** beyond pilot and industrial-heat applications (https://www.energy.gov/edf/energy-storage-projects).

Transmission and land constraints strengthen the firm-resource case. NREL’s 100% clean electricity study modeled total transmission capacity in 2035 at one to almost three times today’s level, implying 1,400–10,100 miles of new high-capacity lines per year if construction starts in 2026 (https://www.energy.gov/cmei/examining-supply-side-options-achieve-100-clean-electricity-2035). Berkeley Lab’s 2025 queue report found about 10,300 active interconnection projects at the end of 2024, representing 1,400 GW of generation and 890 GW of storage, but only 13% of capacity that entered queues from 2000–2019 had reached commercial operation by the end of 2024 (https://eta.lbl.gov/publications/queued-2025-edition-characteristics). NREL also found more than 1,800 wind ordinances and more than 800 solar ordinances in 2022, and extrapolated setback rules could reduce available wind and solar resources by up to 87% and 38%, respectively (https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/impact-of-siting-ordinances-on-land-availability-for-wind-and-sol-2/).

The Clack rebuttal to Jacobson is the canonical critique of 100% WWS overreach. Clack et al. argued Jacobson’s result depended on implausible hydro expansion, unconstrained transmission, unproven multiweek and seasonal storage, free demand shifting, and a model that did not represent power flow, operating reserves, or frequency regulation (https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1610381114). Steelmanned, the critique is not that 100% renewables is physically impossible; it is that proving equivalence to firm nuclear requires validated reliability modeling, commercially demonstrated technologies at scale, plausible deployment rates, and full system cost accounting.

**What The Two Sides Actually Disagree About**

1. Whether the last 5–10% of clean electricity can be supplied cheaper by renewable overbuild, LDES, transmission, and demand flexibility than by firm low-carbon generation. Evidence that would resolve it: audited costs and performance from multi-GW, multi-day LDES fleets plus regional reliability simulations using many decades of weather.

2. Whether U.S. transmission and interconnection can scale at modeled rates. Evidence that would resolve it: sustained completion of thousands of miles per year of high-capacity lines and queue-to-operation timelines falling from over four years toward historical norms (https://eta.lbl.gov/publications/queued-2025-edition-characteristics).

3. Whether nuclear can be built in the U.S. at costs and schedules that beat the renewables-plus-firming alternative. Evidence that would resolve it: repeated U.S. nuclear projects delivered at predictable overnight cost and construction duration, not first-of-a-kind estimates.

4. Whether non-nuclear firm competitors scale first. Evidence that would resolve it: commercial operation of gas+CCS with high capture and low methane leakage, Fervo-scale enhanced geothermal fleets, hydrogen turbines with clean fuel supply, and demand response with verified winter peak performance.

5. Whether social constraints bind more tightly on renewables, nuclear, transmission, or CCS. Evidence that would resolve it: comparable permitting, cancellation, litigation, and community-benefit data across all resource classes.
