**Addendum F: Symmetry Corrections**

As of August 10, 2026, the missing correction is not “nuclear is better” or “renewables are better”; it is that the corpus should treat every pathway as a constrained industrial program, distinguish cheap preservation from growth, and deflate cherry-picked benchmarks.

**1. Existing-Fleet Pathway**

The cheapest nuclear megawatt-hour in the United States is almost certainly not a new reactor; it is keeping already-built reactors online, increasing their licensed output, or restarting recently closed units. NRC defines subsequent license renewal as operation from 60 to 80 years (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/operating/licensing/renewal/subsequent-license-renewal). NRC’s July 2026 SLR table shows 25 U.S. units with completed SLR approvals and 3 units under review: Nine Mile Point 1, Cooper, and Ginna (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/operating/licensing/renewal/subsequent-license-renewal). IEA gives a typical life-extension capital range of about $500-$1,100/kW, producing LCOE generally below $40/MWh, compared with U.S. new-build estimates such as EIA’s $7,821/kW overnight or Vogtle 3-4’s roughly $35 billion for 2,234 MW, about $15,700/kW all-in before normalization (https://www.iea.org/reports/nuclear-power-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary; https://world-nuclear.org/Information-Library/Economic-Aspects/Economics-of-Nuclear-Power).

The pro-nuclear steelman is strong: SLR buys firm, low-carbon generation with known sites, interconnections, workforces, and operating histories. The critic’s steelman is also strong: 80-year operation depends on aging management for equipment that is difficult or impossible to replace, including reactor pressure vessels, concrete containments, buried piping, cables, and reactor internals. NRC itself identifies high-fluence reactor pressure vessel embrittlement, irradiation-assisted stress corrosion cracking, concrete degradation, and cable qualification as SLR technical issues (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/operating/licensing/renewal/slr/background). UCS’s safety critique is that aging can move plants into the “wear-out” region and that some license-renewal safety upgrades are evaluated but not required even when cost-beneficial (https://blog.ucs.org/dlochbaum/nuclear-plant-aging/).

Power uprates are the growth part of the existing fleet. NRC says it had approved 171 uprates by January 2022, adding about 8,030 MWe, equivalent to about eight large reactors (https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/power-uprates). NRC’s 2025 page says uprates have added about 6 GWe in the past 20 years and that industry interest could add another roughly 2 GWe (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/operating/licensing/power-uprates). Cost varies sharply by type: measurement-uncertainty and stretch uprates can be hundreds of dollars per added kW, while extended power uprates can require major turbines, generators, pumps, and transformers; the National Academies gave “hundreds of dollars to about $2,000/kW” for uprates, versus $3,000-$6,000/kW new nuclear in 2007 dollars (https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/12091/chapter/12). Recent paid market data puts case-study uprates at $200-$7,000/kW, with low-cost MUR mostly exhausted and EPUs more variable; treat that as useful but proprietary/secondary (https://thundersaidenergy.com/downloads/nuclear-uprates-capex-costs/).

Restarts are now a real category. Palisades is pursuing restart after permanent shutdown in 2022; DOE has an up-to-$1.52 billion loan guarantee, implying about $1,900/kW if spread across an approximately 800 MW unit, subject to NRC approval (https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-approves-fifth-loan-disbursement-restart-palisades-nuclear-plant; https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/pali). Crane Clean Energy Center, formerly Three Mile Island Unit 1, is an 835 MW restart with a $1 billion DOE loan, but DOE says the loan only partially finances the project, so $1,200/kW is a lower-bound public-finance proxy, not total cost (https://www.energy.gov/edf/crane-restart; https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/ccec). Duane Arnold is a 610 MWe restart candidate with NextEra targeting first-quarter 2029 operation under a Google agreement, but I found no reliable public total capex figure; mark cost-per-kW **UNVERIFIED** (https://www.nrc.gov/info-finder/reactors/duan; https://newsroom.nexteraenergy.com/NextEra-Energy-and-Google-Announce-New-Collaboration-to-Accelerate-Nuclear-Energy-Deployment-in-the-U-S).

Implication: preservation is large; growth is small. U.S. nuclear generated about 781 TWh in 2024 (https://www.eia.gov/todayinEnergy/detail.php?id=64364). SLR can preserve most of that output into the 2030s-2060s, but it does not increase nuclear’s current share. A 2 GWe uprate opportunity at 92% capacity factor is about 16 TWh/year. Palisades, Crane, and Duane Arnold together are about 2.25 GWe, or about 18 TWh/year at 92%. Thus plausible near-term existing-fleet growth is roughly 34 TWh/year, about 4%-5% of current nuclear output and less than 1% of total U.S. generation. Existing-fleet measures are the cheapest nuclear TWh; they cannot by themselves make nuclear “substantially larger” unless the target is preserving share against retirements rather than expanding share.

**2. Alternatives’ Supply Chains**

The prior corpus should not apply industrial skepticism only to nuclear. Gas, grid hardware, batteries, and solar all have gates.

Heavy-duty gas turbines are currently a binding 2030 bottleneck. GE Vernova reported gas-turbine equipment backlog and slot reservations of 116 GW in Q2 2026 and said it was taking reservations for 2031 deliveries (https://www.utilitydive.com/news/ge-vernova-gas-turbine-backlog-climbs-to-116-gw/826039/). Siemens Energy reported a €146 billion order backlog in Q1 FY2026 and 102 gas turbines booked in the quarter (https://www.siemensenergy.com/us/en/home/press-releases/earnings-release-q1-fy-2026.html). Mitsubishi Heavy Industries reported 23 large-frame gas turbine contracts in 1H FY2025, mostly from North America and Asia, after 25 in FY2024 (https://www.mhi.com/news/25110701.html; https://www.mhi.com/news/25050901.html). Industry reports put large-turbine lead times at five-plus years and up to seven years, with new H-class combined-cycle cost estimates around $2,400/kW and some quotes 2.5 times recent prior builds (https://www.spglobal.com/energy/en/news-research/latest-news/electric-power/052025-us-gas-fired-turbine-wait-times-as-much-as-seven-years-costs-up-sharply).

Transformers and switchgear are the hidden constraint for all pathways. DOE says distribution-transformer lead times rose from 3-6 months in 2019 to 12-30 months in 2023 and that U.S. utilities use more than 80,000 transformer varieties (https://www.energy.gov/oe/supply-chain-and-market-analysis). RMI’s 2026 brief says large power transformers can require up to four years and that domestic production met only about 20% of U.S. LPT demand and about 50% of distribution-transformer demand in 2025 (https://rmi.org/resources/solving-the-gridlock-americas-electric-supply-chain-opportunity/). GAO similarly identifies long lead times, limited manufacturing capacity, labor/material shortages, and LPT costs up to $10 million (https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-106180). This binds renewables, gas, nuclear restarts, data centers, and transmission expansion alike.

Battery supply is abundant at global nameplate level but concentrated in China and exposed to trade rules. IEA says global lithium-ion nameplate capacity exceeded 4 TWh by end-2025, while EV battery deployment was 1.2 TWh and EVs were more than 70% of total battery deployment (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries). China accounts for over 80% of global battery manufacturing capacity and over 80% of output, while Chinese producers supplied almost 75% of electric-car battery deployment in 2025 (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries). LFP is now over 55% of EV battery deployment and over 90% of stationary storage installations, but IEA warns LFP cathode materials and precursors remain almost entirely concentrated in China (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries). IEA’s 2025 critical-minerals report says more than 98% of LFP cathode material and LFP battery cells were produced in China in 2024 (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-critical-minerals-outlook-2025/beyond-nmc-batteries-supply-chain-issues-for-emerging-battery-technologies). U.S. policy adds friction: Section 301 tariffs raise lithium-ion non-EV batteries from China to 25% in 2026, and IRA clean-vehicle rules exclude FEOC battery components from 2024 and FEOC critical minerals from 2025 (https://ustr.gov/about-us/policy-offices/press-office/press-releases/2024/may/us-trade-representative-katherine-tai-take-further-action-china-tariffs-after-releasing-statutory; https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy1939).

Solar has the most mature low-cost deployment chain and one of the highest concentration risks. IEA says China’s share exceeds 80% at all solar-panel manufacturing stages and could approach 95% for polysilicon, ingots, and wafers based on capacity under construction; Xinjiang alone accounted for 40% of global polysilicon manufacturing in the cited IEA report (https://www.iea.org/reports/solar-pv-global-supply-chains/executive-summary). DOE says the U.S. solar supply chain is dominated by products from China or companies closely tied to China and that U.S. trade tools include Section 201, Section 301, AD/CVD, tax credits, and loans (https://www.energy.gov/cmei/systems/overview-trade-and-policy-measures-us-solar-manufacturing). DOE also says domestic module capacity could reach about 40 GW by 2026, but domestic demand in 2025-2030 is about 50 GW/year and upstream wafers remain undersupplied near term (https://www.energy.gov/edf/articles/sector-spotlight-solar-pv-supply-chain). UFLPA friction should be treated as material because polysilicon traceability and Xinjiang exposure are directly relevant, though exact detained-MW figures should be flagged **UNVERIFIED** unless the corpus adds CBP shipment data.

Symmetric conclusion: 2030 is hardest for gas turbines, transformers, interconnection, batteries under China/tariff exposure, and solar siting/trade friction. Nuclear new-build is mostly a 2040 question unless projects are already in flight; existing-fleet nuclear is one of the few 2030 nuclear levers. By 2040, gas-turbine and transformer factories can expand, battery and solar supply can diversify, and nuclear can matter if standardized designs and financing are solved. The hardest 2040 gate for nuclear is not uranium; it is repeatable project delivery.

**3. Benchmark Corrections**

Barakah should remain in the benchmark set, but not as a clean $4,000/kW proof point. KEPCO describes Barakah as four APR1400 units totaling 5,600 MW under a 2009 prime contract, including construction, initial fuel, two fuel cycles, and operation support (https://www.kepco.co.kr/eng/business/overseas/business-overview.do). The often-cited $22.4 billion figure implies $4,000/kW, but it is not a U.S.-style all-in regulated-utility cost with financing, owner’s costs, and full local-market labor assumptions. Export credit mattered: Korea Eximbank provided $3.1 billion, while a Korean legal summary says the lender group’s total financing was about $25 billion (https://www.bkl.co.kr/patent/insight/case/486). U.S. Ex-Im also approved $2 billion for U.S. equipment and services, mostly Westinghouse-related, in Barakah (https://www.congress.gov/crs_external_products/R/HTML/R41032.web.html).

The cost deflator also needs a profitability deflator. World Nuclear Industry Status Report 2025, an anti-nuclear source but citing KEPCO business reports, says KEPCO’s cumulative UAE-project profit fell to KRW72.1 billion by end-2024 on KRW22.3 trillion revenue, an implied 0.32% margin, far below the 10% initially expected; use this as a critical claim needing primary KEPCO verification if central to the corpus (https://www.worldnuclearreport.org/World-Nuclear-Industry-Status-Report-2025-HTML-version). The 2012-2013 Korean parts-certification scandal is relevant to supply-chain quality culture, not proof that Barakah is unsafe: WNN reported forged certificates for thousands of mostly non-safety parts in 2012, and Yonhap reported 2013 control-cable certificate fabrication that shut reactors (https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/Korea-probes-forged-quality-certificates; https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20140624002700320). Honest adjusted range: if one adds financing/export-credit economics, owner’s costs, and a normal contractor margin, Barakah is better treated as roughly $5,500-$8,000/kW **UNVERIFIED INFERENCE**, not $4,000/kW. Even doubled to $8,000/kW, it remains far below Vogtle’s roughly $15,700/kW.

Sovacool’s 182,794-fatality accident total needs composition context. The article’s abstract says hydro accidents account for 97.2% of fatalities, nuclear accounts for 90.8% of property damages, and “high accident risk” is a classifier built from historical accident frequency, severity, and property damage, not normalized deaths per TWh or prospective modern risk (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0959652615009877). The 1975 Banqiao dam failure is commonly reported at about 171,000 deaths, which would be about 94% of the 182,794 total; exact table attribution is **UNVERIFIED** from the accessible abstract and should be checked against the article database before reuse.

The nuclear capacity-factor rejoinder should be narrowed. A 92%-93% U.S. fleet capacity factor is a fleet average, not a guarantee that a given unit cannot trip; NEI reports 93.0% for 2023 (https://www.nei.org/resources/statistics/us-nuclear-generating-statistics). A single 1-1.5 GW reactor trip can be the largest contingency for reserve sizing, whereas wind/solar shortfalls are partly forecastable but weather-correlated over broad regions. The standing pro-nuclear counter remains valid: nuclear’s firm capacity contribution is categorically different from weather-correlated variable renewables, even though nuclear is lumpy and has forced outages. Exact U.S. nuclear forced-outage-rate figures should be added from NERC GADS and are **UNVERIFIED** here.

NREL’s 87% wind and 38% solar siting-ordinance figures are not measured current constraints. They are upper-bound scenario results from extrapolating restrictive setbacks nationwide; NREL explicitly says the strictest setback scenario could reduce wind and solar resource by up to 87% and 38%, while still leaving enough technical potential but at different quality/cost (https://www.nrel.gov/grid/news/press/2023/news-release-nrel-analysis-quantifies-impacts-of-setback-ordinances-on-land-available-for-renewable-energy-deployment; https://research-hub.nrel.gov/en/publications/impact-of-siting-ordinances-on-land-availability-for-wind-and-sol-2/).

LCOE lifetime conventions also matter. OECD-NEA/IEA’s 2020 generating-cost report uses discounted cash-flow LCOE and separately finds nuclear long-term operation highly competitive (https://www.iea.org/reports/projected-costs-of-generating-electricity-2020). If a new nuclear plant’s capital recovery is calculated over 60 rather than 30 years, the capital recovery factor falls by about 12% at 7% real discount rate and about 29% at 3%; at 80 years the reductions are about 13% and 34%, respectively. That helps but does not rescue a high-WACC, high-overnight-cost project; discount rate and construction duration remain larger drivers.

**4. Missing Literatures**

The strongest pro-renewables anti-nuclear argument is opportunity cost: Lovins/RMI argues that nuclear is slow and capital-intensive, so the same dollar and year spent on efficiency, wind, solar, demand flexibility, and distributed resources can avoid more carbon sooner (https://rmi.org/nuclearwhyeventhinkaboutit/; https://rmi.org/resources/fourteen-alleged-magical-properties-coal-nuclear-plants-dont-shouldnt-paid-extra-providing/). The pro-nuclear reply is that this can become a false marginal comparison when systems need firm, low-carbon capacity, not just annual MWh.

Sovacool, Stirling et al.’s Nature Energy 2020 claim should be included: national “attachment” to nuclear was not associated with significantly lower carbon emissions, while renewables were, and nuclear-renewables strategies appeared negatively associated in their dataset (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41560-020-00696-3). The rebuttal literature argues the result is sensitive to model specification, historical path dependence, and omitted variables, and that nuclear and renewables can both associate with decarbonization; add Fell et al. and related “Matters Arising” rebuttals with primary DOIs before coding this dispute as settled. Exact rebuttal DOI list is **UNVERIFIED** in this addendum.

Non-Jacobson high-renewables modeling should be represented. Vibrant Clean Energy’s WIS:dom work generally claims high distributed solar, storage, flexible demand, and transmission can lower system cost; LUT/Breyer studies generally claim 100% renewable global systems are technically feasible with storage, power-to-X, transmission, and some residual firm capacity from hydro/biomass/synthetic fuels depending on region. Exact firm-capacity residuals are **UNVERIFIED** here and should be entered model-by-model, not as “100% renewables need no firming.” Jacobson v. Clack also belongs in a factual paragraph: Jacobson sued Clack and NAS/PNAS for defamation after Clack’s PNAS critique of Jacobson’s 100% renewables paper, sought large damages, voluntarily withdrew, and D.C. courts later affirmed anti-SLAPP fee awards to the defendants (https://app.midpage.ai/case/jacobson-v-clack-9940749; https://retractionwatch.com/2021/11/19/stanford-prof-fights-efforts-to-make-him-pay-at-least-75000-in-legal-fees-after-dropping-defamation-suit/).

The corpus also needs pro-LNT evidence. INWORKS 2023 followed 309,932 nuclear workers in France, the U.K., and the U.S. and reported a positive dose-response for solid-cancer mortality, with full-cohort ERR about 0.52 per Gy colon dose in the PubMed summary; this is the strongest recent counterweight to hormesis/threshold critiques (https://www.bmj.com/content/382/bmj-2022-074520/article-info; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40957622/).

Germany’s phase-out should not be reduced to one counterfactual. Jarvis/Deschenes/Jha estimate the post-Fukushima shutdowns were replaced mainly by coal and imports and cost about $12 billion/year in external damages (https://www.nber.org/papers/w26598). The critique is that later German coal fell anyway as renewables, imports, gas-price dynamics, and demand changed. German coal generation fell from roughly 270 TWh in 2015 to roughly 95-130 TWh in 2024 depending on gross/net and public/all-generation accounting; exact series should be locked to Ember or Fraunhofer before final corpus coding **UNVERIFIED** (https://www.energy-charts.info/charts/energy/chart.htm?l=en&c=DE&year=-1; https://ember-energy.org/countries-and-regions/countries/germany/). RTE-style clarity is needed: state whether the question is “what replaced nuclear in the hour/year of closure?” or “did the national system decarbonize over a decade?”

Climate stress cuts both ways. France’s 2022 nuclear collapse was mostly corrosion/maintenance rather than heat, with RTE reporting nuclear output of 279 TWh versus 360.7 TWh in 2021 and average availability of 54% versus 73% in 2015-2019 (https://analysesetdonnees.rte-france.com/en/electricity-review-keyfindings). Heatwaves and low rivers did trigger local derates and discharge-temperature waivers, but the precise 2022 heat-only TWh loss is **UNVERIFIED** here. U.S. coastal and drought exposure should include Turkey Point sea-level/hurricane debates, but site-specific quantified risk is **UNVERIFIED** without NRC/environmental-review extraction. Battery counterweights also need inclusion: power capacity is not energy capacity, degradation means augmentation or replacement, and fires are low-probability but real operational risks, illustrated by the January 2025 Moss Landing fire **UNVERIFIED citation pending primary incident record**.

**Revised Disagreement Propositions**

1. Existing-fleet nuclear should be evaluated separately from new-build nuclear: it is low-cost preservation plus modest growth, not a stand-alone route to a much larger nuclear share.

2. The decisive 2030 bottlenecks are industrial and grid-facing: gas turbines, transformers, interconnection, battery China exposure, solar trade/siting, and nuclear restart licensing.

3. International nuclear benchmarks resolve only if costs are normalized for financing, owner’s costs, labor model, quality culture, and contractor margin; Barakah remains impressive after adjustment, but not directly portable to U.S. megaprojects.

4. The core modeling dispute is not “firm vs variable” in the abstract; it is what mix of firm capacity, transmission, storage duration, demand flexibility, permitting success, and cost of capital clears reliability at least cost under U.S. political constraints.
