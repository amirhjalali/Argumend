**ADDENDUM E: Missing Lenses**

**1. Fuel-Cycle Front End**
The U.S. uranium legacy is not an abstraction: from 1944 to 1986, nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands, and EPA now identifies 523 abandoned uranium mines on or near Navajo Nation land, spread across more than 27,000 square miles in Arizona, New Mexico, and Utah (https://www.epa.gov/enforcement/case-summary-600-million-settlement-clean-94-abandoned-uranium-mines-navajo-nation). EPA says federal agencies have worked with Navajo Nation agencies since 2008 and have secured enforcement agreements and settlements valued at more than $1.7 billion, with funds available to begin assessment and cleanup at 230 of the 523 abandoned mines (https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/aum-cleanup). EPA also prioritized 46 mines based on gamma radiation, proximity to homes, and water-contamination potential, and reported in 2026 that 44 priority mines remained in the assessment phase (https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/aum-cleanup). The justice claim is therefore concrete: the first U.S. nuclear age concentrated extraction burdens on Native communities while the benefits flowed to federal weapons programs and later national power systems (https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P1019V8Q.txt).

The July 16, 1979 Church Rock tailings dam failure at United Nuclear Corporation’s mill released about 94 million gallons of acidic radioactive liquid and about 1,100 tons of tailings into the Rio Puerco system; it occurred less than four months after the March 28, 1979 Three Mile Island accident, and is widely described as the largest radioactive-material release in U.S. history by volume, but exact “more than TMI” radiological-dose comparisons vary by source and should be treated as **UNVERIFIED** unless tied to a specific isotope and dose pathway (https://www.epa.gov/superfund/united-nuclear; https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/3mile-isle.html). Downstream Navajo communities used the Rio Puerco corridor for livestock and household purposes, and the spill became a paradigmatic environmental-justice event because the immediate hazard was not a reactor accident but front-end waste management (https://www.epa.gov/superfund/united-nuclear).

RECA partially acknowledged this weapons-era burden, but its coverage was historically limited: CRS described benefits of $75,000 for onsite atmospheric-test participants, $50,000 for eligible downwinders, and $100,000 for uranium miners, millers, and ore transporters who worked from 1942 through 1971 and developed covered diseases (https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R43956). RECA authorization ended June 7, 2024, with DOJ processing claims postmarked by June 10, 2024, before Congress later reauthorized RECA through Public Law 119-21, enacted July 4, 2025, with DOJ listing a December 31, 2027 filing deadline and revised regulations expected during 2026 (https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R43956; https://www.justice.gov/civil/reca; https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/COMPS-707). A 2025 Senate bill sought broader expansion, including post-1971 uranium work, core drilling, and Manhattan Project waste communities, but Congress.gov lists S.243 as introduced and referred to Senate Judiciary on January 24, 2025 rather than enacted as a standalone bill (https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/243).

A key correction for the base corpus is that common “deaths per TWh” comparisons do not fully internalize this legacy. Our World in Data says its energy death-rate comparison includes deaths from air pollution and accidents in the supply chain, but its nuclear death-rate method divides assumed Chernobyl and Fukushima deaths by cumulative global nuclear generation, while its lifecycle-emissions comparison separately includes mining, transport, and maintenance emissions (https://ourworldindata.org/nuclear-energy). That boundary means Navajo uranium-mining disease, abandoned-mine exposure, and tailings harms are not comprehensively represented in the headline nuclear deaths-per-TWh figure unless they appear in the underlying accident database and are attributed to nuclear supply chains (https://ourworldindata.org/nuclear-energy). The pro-nuclear reply is not that these harms were imaginary; it is that they belong to a historically specific weapons-procurement and early-regulation era and should not be mechanically assigned to all modern nuclear electricity.

The modern counterpoint is material. U.S. nuclear operators purchased 55.9 million pounds U3O8e in 2024, and EIA reported that 36% came from Canada, 24% from Kazakhstan, 17% from Australia, 9% from Uzbekistan, 4% from Namibia, 4% from Russia, and 8% from U.S.-origin uranium (https://www.eia.gov/uranium/marketing/?stream=top). U.S. mine production rose to 657,000 pounds U3O8 in 2024 and 2.109 million pounds in 2025, still far below 2024 reactor purchases (https://www.eia.gov/uranium/production/annual/index.php). USGS reported that 2024 U.S. production represented less than 0.5% of global output and about 1.4% of uranium required for U.S. commercial reactors (https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/fs20253057/full). ISR mining has a different risk profile than legacy open-pit and underground mining because it leaves ore underground, uses injection and recovery wells, creates little surface disturbance, and generates no conventional tailings pile, though groundwater restoration and brine disposal remain central risks (https://world-nuclear.org/information-library/Nuclear-Fuel-Cycle/Mining-of-Uranium/In-Situ-Leach-Mining-of-Uranium). A large U.S. nuclear expansion could increase domestic mining pressure, especially after the Russian uranium import ban enacted May 13, 2024, but EIA’s 2025 data show standby ISR capacity of 8.8 million pounds per year and planned ISR capacity of 10.5 million pounds per year, which is meaningful but not enough by itself to cover current U.S. demand, much less expansion demand (https://www.energy.gov/articles/biden-harris-administration-enacts-law-banning-importation-russian-uranium; https://www.eia.gov/uranium/production/annual/index.php).

**2. Consent, Siting, And Who Decides**
Yucca Mountain is not just a geology dispute. Western Shoshone opponents ground their objection in the 1863 Treaty of Ruby Valley, arguing that Yucca Mountain lies within Newe Sogobia and that the treaty did not cede land for a national nuclear-waste repository (https://digitreaties.org/treaties/treaty/178907585/; https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914b5d5add7b04934775446). Nevada’s formal objection had a statutory path: after the Secretary of Energy recommended Yucca Mountain on February 14, 2002, Nevada submitted a notice of disapproval, and Congress overrode it by joint resolution, with House passage 306-117 and Senate passage 60-39 before Public Law 107-200 affirmed the site (https://www.energy.gov/management/february-14-2002-yucca-mountain; https://energycommerce.house.gov/yucca-mountain). That procedure created legality without host-state consent.

Consolidated interim storage repeated the legitimacy problem. NRC licensed Interim Storage Partners’ Andrews County, Texas CISF in September 2021 for an initial 5,000 metric tons uranium of spent fuel over 40 years, with later amendments contemplated up to 40,000 metric tons uranium (https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/staff/sr2239/). Texas enacted a 2021 law barring acceptance of high-level radioactive waste for storage at a consolidated interim storage facility, and state officials opposed the project (https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/87R/billtext/html/HB04095I.htm; https://www.texastribune.org/2021/09/10/texas-nuclear-waste-ban/). NRC licensed Holtec’s HI-STORE CISF in Lea County, New Mexico in 2023 for 8,680 metric tons uranium over 40 years, and New Mexico enacted SB 53 in 2023 barring permits for spent fuel or high-level waste storage unless the state consents and a permanent repository is operating (https://thefederalregister.org/documents/2023-10162/holtec-international-hi-store-consolidated-interim-storage-facility; https://www.nmlegis.gov/Sessions/23%20Regular/bills/senate/SB0053.html). In NRC v. Texas, decided June 18, 2025, the Supreme Court held 6-3 that entities that were not parties to the NRC licensing proceeding could not obtain Hobbs Act judicial review of the licensing decision; the Court did not squarely decide that every private away-from-reactor CISF is substantively lawful under the Nuclear Waste Policy Act (https://www.scotusblog.com/cases/interim-storage-partners-llc-v-texas/; https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-1300). Holtec later canceled its New Mexico land-use arrangement in October 2025, citing an untenable path forward amid state opposition (https://www.ans.org/news/2025-10-10/article-7449/holtec-pulls-out-of-new-mexico-snf-interim-storage-project/).

The tribal-land siting record cuts both ways. The Skull Valley Band of Goshute Indians negotiated with Private Fuel Storage beginning in the 1990s, NRC’s 2001 EIS evaluated an ISFSI on the reservation, and the project died after Utah opposition, federal land and lease barriers, and PFS’s 2012 request to terminate the NRC license (https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/staff/sr1714/v1/index; https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/359/536/577495/; https://archive.sltrib.com/article.php?id=55513674&itype=CMSID). Prairie Island shows the opposite pattern: a tribe living next to a plant and spent-fuel site it says was approved without tribal consideration in 1973, later receiving statutory settlement payments that Minnesota law now lists at $7.5 million per year plus $50,000 per dry cask if a qualifying amended settlement exists (https://prairieisland.org/our-government/priorities; https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/2025/cite/216B.1645).

DOE’s consent-based siting program is real but early. DOE awarded $26 million in June 2023 to 13 consortia, said it was in the planning-and-capacity-building stage, and stated it was not yet soliciting volunteer host communities (https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USEERE/bulletins/35f2ffe). DOE’s consortia page later listed 12 awardees, roughly $2 million per awardee, 252 public engagements, 18 community grants, 16 tribal engagements, 24 information-resource projects, and 40 government engagements as of December 2024 (https://www.energy.gov/ne/consent-based-siting-consortia). Critics from the anti-CISF side argue interim storage risks becoming de facto permanent without an operating repository and can reproduce environmental injustice in rural, poor, minority, or Native communities (https://ieer.org/resource/repository/comments-doe-consent-based-siting-nuclear-waste-2016/). Critics from the pro-nuclear side argue DOE is still not selecting sites, not defining consent tightly enough, and not solving the 90,000 metric tons of spent fuel DOE says is stored at more than 70 U.S. reactor sites, including 20 sites with no operating reactor (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/department-energy-moves-forward-consolidated-interim-storage-facility-project-spent).

Steelman against expansion: no buildout is legitimate until affected host communities, states, and tribes have a durable veto or negotiated-consent role, because Yucca Mountain, Prairie Island, Navajo mining, Texas CISF, and New Mexico CISF show repeated siting without the consent of those asked to bear concentrated burdens (https://energycommerce.house.gov/yucca-mountain; https://prairieisland.org/our-government/priorities; https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/aum-cleanup). Steelman for expansion: consent processes now exist, host communities sometimes support nuclear facilities, and a 2022 Bisconti survey reported 91% favorable views of local nuclear plants among residents within 10 miles of operating U.S. plants, 88% positive views of nuclear energy, 78% support for adding a reactor, and 86% support for adding an SMR (https://www.ans.org/news/article-4228/public-support-for-nuclear-energy-is-highest-among-plant-neighbors/). The pro-expansion procedural point is also comparative: fossil, transmission, wind, solar, and mining projects often proceed through permits and litigation rather than unanimous local consent, so applying an absolute consent rule only to nuclear is itself a normative choice, not an evidence finding.

**3. Normative Frameworks**
Per-TWh accounting answers “how many deaths per unit of energy under a selected boundary,” but rights-based critics ask whether some risks should not be imposed without consent even when expected deaths are low. Kristin Shrader-Frechette argues that geological disposal policy is scientifically overconfident because 10,000-year containment predictions are uncertain and ethically deficient because it can violate equal treatment, due process, informed consent, and duties to future generations (https://www.ucpress.edu/books/burying-uncertainty/paper; https://academic.oup.com/book/6860/chapter/151065305). Her position is not merely that nuclear is scary; it is that risk transfer to politically weaker present communities and nonconsenting future persons can be unjust even when a utilitarian ledger is favorable (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/0272-4332.206071).

Charles Perrow’s Normal Accidents thesis claims that complex, tightly coupled systems can produce accidents through unanticipated interactions that operators cannot understand quickly enough, and he treated nuclear power as a core example of such a system (https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/normal-accidents-living-high-risk-technologies). After Fukushima, Perrow argued that even strong regulation and training cannot eliminate rare cascading failures in some high-consequence systems because interactive complexity and tight coupling are structural properties, not merely management defects (https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0096340211426395). Scott Sagan’s Limits of Safety applies a similar organizational lens to nuclear weapons: redundancy can create hidden common-mode failures, tight coupling can accelerate failure propagation, and parochial organizational interests can constrain learning from near misses (https://www.osti.gov/biblio/549192; https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/limits-safety-organizations-accidents-and-nuclear-weapons). The relevance to civilian nuclear is analogical rather than identical, so claims importing Sagan directly from weapons command-and-control to civilian reactors should be labeled **UNVERIFIED** unless the institutional mechanism is specified.

Discounting is another unresolved normative fault line. OMB’s Circular A-4 formula discounts a future cost by 1/(1+r)^t, so a $1 harm 10,000 years from now has present value of about 4.3e-129 dollars at 3% and about 1.5e-294 dollars at 7% by direct application of OMB’s formula (https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/omb/circulars_a004_a-4). That arithmetic makes deep-time waste harms almost vanish in standard NPV, which is why discounting debates in climate and environmental policy are explicitly ethical as well as empirical (https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-environ-020420-042100). Intergenerational-equity scholars argue that date of birth alone should not radically reduce moral weight, while critics reply that far-future preferences, technologies, wealth, and opportunity costs are uncertain, and over-protecting the far future can underfund preventable present harms (https://www.lse.ac.uk/granthaminstitute/publication/ethics-equity-and-the-economics-of-climate-change-paper-2-economics-and-politics-working-paper-84b/; https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0047272718300380).

The strongest precautionary nuclear argument says society should not expand a technology with low-probability but irreversible contamination, weapons-linkage, or waste-governance failure until proponents prove institutions can maintain safety and consent across accident, war, and political-decay scenarios. The Sunstein-style critique is that strong precaution is paralyzing because blocking nuclear increases fossil and climate risks, and precaution must compare risks of action with risks of inaction rather than privileging the most vivid tail (https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/laws-of-fear/precautions-and-paralysis/0F2D9257D652D2C88263C7BDD67948CB; https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/law_and_economics/286/). Evidence can reduce uncertainty about accident frequencies, health effects, repository performance, and fuel supply, but evidence cannot resolve whether consent is a side constraint, whether future persons have equal claims, or how to compare catastrophic low-probability harms with near-term fossil mortality.

**4. Energy Security And Geopolitics**
Energy-security arguments are independent of plant economics. IEA reported that of 52 reactor construction starts worldwide since 2017, 25 used Chinese designs and 23 used Russian designs, meaning 48 of 52, or 92%, used Chinese or Russian technology (https://www.iea.org/reports/the-path-to-a-new-era-for-nuclear-energy/executive-summary). IEA’s 2026 review says 94% of reactors that started construction over the past decade were of Chinese or Russian design (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2026/technology-nuclear). WNISR reported that as of July 1, 2025, Rosatom was constructing 27 of 63 reactors under construction worldwide, including 20 abroad in seven countries (https://www.worldnuclearreport.org/World-Nuclear-Industry-Status-Report-2025-HTML-version). China’s global export footprint is smaller than Russia’s because most Chinese builds are domestic, but IEA reported that Pakistan began construction in 2024 on the first Generation III+ Chinese-origin reactor outside China (https://www.iea.org/reports/global-energy-review-2025/electricity).

The pro-nuclear statecraft argument is that reactor exports create 60- to 100-year relationships through construction, fuel, maintenance, training, and decommissioning, and U.S. absence cedes safety, security, and safeguards norm-setting to Rosatom and Chinese state firms (https://www.csis.org/analysis/geopolitics-russias-civil-nuclear-exports-four-years-war; https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/nuclear-nexus-export-control-compliance). Akkuyu is the concrete example: Turkey’s $20 billion plant is described as the first Rosatom build-own-operate nuclear plant, a structure that raises concerns about long-term Russian influence over Turkish energy infrastructure (https://jamestown.org/program/turkiye-builds-nuclear-plant-with-russia-to-boost-energy-security/). The domestic-security argument adds that the United States imported 81% of enrichment services in 2024, including 20% from Russia, while DOE says U.S. reactors historically imported 20-25% of enriched uranium from Russia and that the 2024 Russian LEU ban runs from August 2024 through December 31, 2040 with waivers through 2027 (https://www.eia.gov/uranium/marketing/?stream=top; https://www.energy.gov/ne/domestic-low-enriched-uranium-supply-chain). Nuclear also has a resilience claim because reactors refuel every 18 to 24 months and DOE describes nuclear energy as resilient and reliable, though resilience is system-specific rather than automatic (https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=1490; https://www.energy.gov/ne/office-nuclear-energy).

The counterargument is that domestic buildout is neither necessary nor sufficient for export competitiveness. Russia’s advantage is state finance and turnkey diplomacy, China’s advantage is large domestic build rates and industrial policy, and U.S. deployment alone does not create export financing, fuel-service capacity, or recipient-country appetite. Alliance supply already covers much of U.S. need: EIA reported 2024 uranium deliveries from Canada, Kazakhstan, Australia, Uzbekistan, Namibia, and Russia, and enrichment shares from France, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, and Germany in addition to Russia and U.S.-origin SWU (https://www.eia.gov/uranium/marketing/?stream=top). Export advocacy can therefore be genuine statecraft, but it can also be rent-seeking if “countering Russia and China” becomes a blank check for uneconomic domestic projects without clear export, safeguards, or fuel-cycle deliverables.

**Closing: New Disagreement Propositions**
1. **PROCEDURAL**: A nuclear expansion is legitimate only if host communities, tribes, and states have a durable right to refuse mines, waste sites, and reactors. This cannot be resolved by risk data alone; it requires a rule of political authority and consent.

2. **BOTH**: Communities that accept nuclear burdens are owed enforceable benefits, monitoring, emergency capacity, and exit terms, not merely hearings. Evidence can price some burdens, but it cannot determine what level of compensation makes imposed risk legitimate.

3. **NORMATIVE**: A death prevented this decade and a contamination risk imposed 10,000 years from now should not be compared using ordinary NPV alone. Evidence can inform magnitudes; moral theory must decide discounting.

4. **BOTH**: The United States should subsidize domestic nuclear only if the subsidy demonstrably improves safety, safeguards, fuel security, or export leverage. Evidence can test deliverables; values decide whether statecraft justifies costs.

5. **PROCEDURAL**: Interim storage should not proceed before a credible permanent-repository path exists. Evidence can assess repository feasibility; governance must decide whether “interim” without a destination is an acceptable promise.
