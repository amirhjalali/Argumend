**Research Report: Can New Nuclear Plants Be Constructed Economically in the U.S.?**

As of August 10, 2026, the strongest evidence against U.S. new-build nuclear economics is the realized cost record of the last U.S. projects. Vogtle Units 3 and 4, two AP1000 reactors totaling about 2,234 MWe, entered commercial operation on July 31, 2023 and April 29, 2024, respectively, and World Nuclear Association summarizes their total cost at “about $35 billion,” implying roughly $15,700/kW before any adjustment for net/gross capacity conventions (https://world-nuclear.org/Information-Library/Economic-Aspects/Economics-of-Nuclear-Power). The original Vogtle 3 and 4 estimate was about $14 billion, so the completed project cost was about 2.5 times the starting estimate and roughly $21 billion over the initial budget (https://www.worldnuclearreport.org/The-World-Nuclear-Industry-Status-Report-2018-HTML). A stricter all-in press/accounting estimate of roughly $36 billion implies about $16,100/kW, but I could not verify one single official all-owner “final cost” filing that reconciles all owners, financing, Toshiba payments, and cost-sharing adjustments in one table, so the exact final all-in denominator should be treated as partly **UNVERIFIED** beyond the cited $35 billion summary (https://world-nuclear.org/Information-Library/Economic-Aspects/Economics-of-Nuclear-Power).

The companion U.S. AP1000 project, V.C. Summer Units 2 and 3, was abandoned in July 2017 after the owners had spent about $9 billion, and the NRC says the combined licenses were terminated on March 6, 2019 (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/large-lwr/col/summer). Congressional Research Service reported that completion estimates had risen from an initial $9.8 billion for both units to $17.9 billion excluding Santee Cooper’s interest calculation before abandonment (https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN10750.html). The South Carolina Attorney General later said the Base Load Review Act allowed nine rate increases from 2009 to 2017 for the project before it produced electricity, which illustrates how construction risk was shifted toward ratepayers (https://www.scag.gov/about-the-office/news/former-scana-ceo-kevin-marsh-pleads-guilty-to-state-grand-jury-indictment-related-to-v-c-summer-nuclear-power-plant-project/).

Non-U.S. comparisons show both sides of the case. France’s Flamanville 3 EPR was estimated by the Cour des comptes at €23.7 billion in 2023 euros including financing, first-operation costs, decommissioning provisions, and waste-management provisions, while EDF’s construction-cost estimate was €15.6 billion in 2023 euros (https://www.ccomptes.fr/fr/documents/73293). Using about 1.63 GWe for Flamanville 3, the Cour des comptes all-in figure is roughly €14,500/kW, which is Vogtle-like rather than Korea-like (https://www.sfeninenglish.org/french-court-auditors-epr-sector-report/). Hinkley Point C’s latest EDF public update put completion cost at £31 billion to £34 billion in 2015 pounds, excluding interim interest, with Unit 1 scenarios ranging from 2029 to 2031 (https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2024/01/23/2814426/0/en/hinkley-point-c-update.html). Barakah in the UAE is the counterexample often cited by nuclear optimists: four Korean APR1400 units totaling 5.6 GWe were reported in a peer-reviewed source as costing $22.4 billion, or about $4,000/kW gross (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2021/3133433). The IAEA PRIS database lists all four Barakah units as operational by 2024, with 5,348 MWe net capacity and 42,379 GWh supplied in 2025 (https://pris.iaea.org/PRIS/CountryStatistics/CountryDetails.aspx?current=AE).

LCOE comparisons cut against new nuclear when read narrowly. Lazard’s latest public report is LCOE+ Version 18.0, released June 16, 2025 (https://www.lazard.com/news-announcements/lazard-releases-2025-levelized-cost-of-energyplus-report-pr/). Lazard reports unsubsidized utility-scale solar at $38-$78/MWh, onshore wind at $37-$86/MWh, and U.S. nuclear at $141-$220/MWh, with the nuclear estimate based on Vogtle-derived public cost data and inflation-adjusted older Lazard assumptions (https://www.lazard.com/media/5tlbhyla/lazards-lcoeplus-june-2025-_vf.pdf). EIA’s AEO2026 LCOE report gives 2031-entry average LCOE estimates of $87.81/MWh for advanced nuclear, $58.33/MWh for solar PV, $56.75/MWh for onshore wind, $94.20/MWh for PV-battery hybrid, and $77.46/MWh for combined-cycle gas in the Counterfactual Baseline case (https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/aeo/electricity_generation/pdf/LCOE_report.pdf). EIA explicitly warns that LCOE alone does not capture all investment factors, and it pairs LCOE with LACE and value-cost ratios because grid value differs by dispatchability, timing, location, and reliability contribution (https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/aeo/electricity_generation/pdf/LCOE_report.pdf). NREL similarly defines LCOE as an energy-cost metric and warns that it does not necessarily show the lowest-cost option for the grid in a specific place and time (https://atb-archive.nrel.gov/electricity/2020/definitions.php).

The strongest “nuclear can become economical” case is not that Vogtle was cheap, but that Vogtle was a first-of-a-kind U.S. restart project whose pathologies are avoidable. MIT’s AP1000 assessment argued that Vogtle’s overnight capital cost was about $7,956/kW in 2018 dollars, but that a next U.S. AP1000 series could plausibly fall to about $4,300/kW and a 10th unit to about $2,900/kW if deployed serially with stable designs and competent project delivery (https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/Articles/AP1000-remains-attractive-option-for-US-market-say). Lovering, Yip, and Nordhaus found that nuclear construction-cost histories differ strongly by country and that South Korea had sustained cost reductions rather than inevitable cost escalation (https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/record.jsf?pid=diva2%3A2023071). The pro-nuclear steelman says the U.S. has been paying for discontinuity: no recent workforce, incomplete design at construction start, vendor bankruptcy, fragmented supply chains, and a regulatory/construction learning curve restarted from cold rather than from a live program (https://www.worldnuclearreport.org/The-World-Nuclear-Industry-Status-Report-2018-HTML). In that view, the relevant benchmark is not Vogtle as a one-off but Barakah, South Korea, China, or a repeated AP1000/BWRX-300/Natrium program with factory repetition, multi-unit sites, and lower financing spreads (https://world-nuclear.org/Information-Library/Economic-Aspects/Economics-of-Nuclear-Power).

The strongest skeptic case is that U.S. nuclear’s problem is structural, not accidental. Grubler’s study of the French build-out found “negative learning” even in a centralized, standardized, state-backed program, meaning costs rose in real terms despite scale (https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/enepol/v38y2010i9p5174-5188.html). Critics of optimistic overnight-cost learning curves argue that overnight costs omit delay, financing, contingency, and institutional risk that dominate real nuclear project economics (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421518303446). The skeptic steelman says the U.S. combines high labor costs, adversarial contracting, scarce nuclear-grade suppliers, high interest rates, long licensing/construction durations, and a political system that cannot credibly commit to serial builds, so “Nth-of-a-kind” cost claims are not financeable until many expensive “first” units are already complete (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0301421518303446). On this view, Vogtle and V.C. Summer are not outliers; they are the revealed U.S. delivery system.

SMRs sharpen the dispute because they promise smaller balance-sheet risk but sacrifice economies of unit scale. NuScale and UAMPS terminated the Carbon Free Power Project on November 8, 2023 because subscriptions were insufficient (https://www.nuscalepower.com/press-releases/2023/utah-associated-municipal-power-systems-and-nuscale-power-agree-to-terminate-the-carbon-free-power-project). Public reporting put NuScale CFPP’s cost escalation at roughly $5.3 billion to $9.2 billion and its target power price at roughly $58/MWh to $89/MWh after subsidies; I verified these numbers only through live secondary reporting, so they should be treated as **UNVERIFIED by primary source** here (https://smrintel.com/nuscale-what-happened/). The credible skeptic case is that SMRs may be too small to benefit from scale, too novel to avoid FOAK risk, and too order-dependent to achieve factory learning without a large committed order book. The credible pro-SMR case is that smaller modules can use more standardized factories, shorter construction schedules, brownfield sites, and lower absolute capital commitments, which may lower financing risk even if $/kW is not immediately lower.

Current SMR status is mixed but materially more active than in 2020. NRC issued TerraPower’s Kemmerer Natrium construction permit on March 9, 2026, and DOE describes Natrium as a 345 MWe sodium-cooled fast reactor with molten-salt storage that can boost output to 500 MWe for several hours (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nrc-issues-construction-permit-terrapowers-natrium-advanced-reactor). X-energy and Dow received an NRC environmental assessment and finding of no significant impact for the proposed Seadrift, Texas Xe-100 project in May 2026 (https://x-energy.com/news/nrc-issues-environmental-assessment-with-finding-of-no-significant-impact-for-dow-and-x-energys-propsed-advanced-nuclear-project-in-texas/). Westinghouse AP300 remains in NRC pre-application activity, not construction, as of the NRC page updated June 2026 (https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/who-were-working-with/pre-application-activities/westinghouse). The BWRX-300 is furthest along in Canada, where CNSC authorized OPG to construct one Darlington unit in April 2025, and TVA submitted a U.S. construction-permit application for a BWRX-300 at Clinch River in 2025 with a targeted NRC decision in fall 2026 (https://www.canada.ca/en/nuclear-safety-commission/news/2025/04/commission-authorizes-ontario-power-generation-inc-to-construct-1-bwrx-300-reactor-at-the-darlington-new-nuclear-project-site.html; https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/new-reactors/advanced/who-were-working-with/applicant-projects/clinch-river).

Financing may be the decisive variable. EIA’s AEO2026 LCOE assumes a 30-year cost recovery period and 7.27% after-tax WACC for the 2031 online year, and capital-intensive technologies such as nuclear are especially sensitive to WACC because most costs are paid before revenue begins (https://www.eia.gov/outlooks/aeo/electricity_generation/pdf/LCOE_report.pdf). The IRA created the 45U zero-emission nuclear production credit for existing nuclear electricity sold after December 31, 2023 and before January 1, 2033 (https://www.irs.gov/credits-deductions/zero-emission-nuclear-power-production-credit). DOE says new advanced nuclear can qualify for technology-neutral zero-emission production and investment credits under the IRA, and it also notes $2.5 billion from the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law for the Advanced Reactor Demonstration Program (https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/biden-harris-administration-takes-action-secure-nuclear-fuel-supply-chain-equip). The ADVANCE Act of 2024 directed NRC modernization and advanced-reactor licensing changes, but its economic effect depends on whether shorter and more predictable licensing actually reduces project-finance risk (https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/senate-bill/870).

Recent hyperscaler deals are evidence of demand for firm clean power, but not yet proof of economical new construction. Microsoft and Constellation announced a 20-year power purchase agreement tied to restarting Three Mile Island Unit 1 as the Crane Clean Energy Center, but the public announcement did not disclose the PPA price (https://www.constellationenergy.com/newsroom/2024/Constellation-to-Launch-Crane-Clean-Energy-Center-Restoring-Jobs-and-Carbon-Free-Power-to-The-Grid.html). Google and Kairos announced a deal targeting 500 MW of advanced nuclear capacity by 2035, but the public announcement did not disclose prices (https://blog.google/outreach-initiatives/sustainability/google-kairos-power-nuclear-energy-agreement/). Amazon announced agreements involving X-energy and Energy Northwest SMRs, but the public announcement did not disclose a delivered $/MWh price (https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/sustainability/amazon-nuclear-small-modular-reactor-net-carbon-zero). These deals support the claim that some buyers value 24/7 clean firm power above generic renewable LCOE, but undisclosed pricing leaves the central economics question unresolved.

**Neutral Disagreement Propositions**

1. Whether Vogtle and V.C. Summer are representative of future U.S. nuclear delivery or one-time restart failures. Evidence that would resolve it: signed fixed-price or transparently audited U.S. nuclear projects delivered under $6,000/kW and within 10% of schedule.

2. Whether serial construction can overcome U.S. institutional cost drivers. Evidence that would resolve it: a multi-unit U.S. AP1000, BWRX-300, AP300, or Natrium sequence showing measured learning across units.

3. Whether SMRs lower total project risk enough to offset weaker unit-scale economics. Evidence that would resolve it: first commercial SMRs with audited overnight cost, financing cost, capacity factor, and realized PPA price.

4. Whether firm clean capacity value justifies nuclear’s higher LCOE. Evidence that would resolve it: market-clearing 24/7 clean-energy contracts or capacity-expansion studies comparing nuclear against renewables, storage, transmission, gas with CCS, geothermal, and demand response on a full-system basis.

5. Whether policy can reduce nuclear cost or only shift it. Evidence that would resolve it: projects whose unsubsidized cost falls, not merely projects made financeable through tax credits, loan guarantees, or ratepayer risk transfer.
