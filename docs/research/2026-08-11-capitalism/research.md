**Research Report**

The definitional issue comes first. If “capitalism survived” means private ownership of productive assets, profit-seeking firms, enforceable property rights, and prices allocating capital and labor, then AI can coexist with a much larger welfare state, public compute, antitrust, and social dividends while remaining capitalist (https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/series/back-to-basics/capitalism; https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/markets/). If “survived” means most households obtain purchasing power mainly by selling labor in competitive labor markets, then advanced AI is a much deeper threat. A lot of disagreement is really about which definition is being used.

Capitalism structurally needs five things: a broad income-distribution channel, effective consumer demand, price signals, property rights, and competition. The labor market has been the main income-distribution channel in modern capitalism: firms pay wages, households buy output, and capital owners receive residual profits. AI threatens this channel only if it substitutes for labor across a wide enough task frontier that displaced workers cannot re-enter higher-productivity complementary jobs. That is the causal chain: task automation -> lower marginal product of broad labor categories -> falling wage bill/labor share -> weaker mass purchasing power unless replaced by transfers, capital ownership, new work, or cheaper goods. This is plausible under “transformative AI” scenarios, but not established by current evidence; the claim that AI will perform virtually all economically valuable human work remains **UNVERIFIED** (https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/scenario-planning-for-an-agi-future-anton-korinek).

Price signals are less directly broken. Hayek’s classic argument is that prices compress dispersed information into decentralized allocation signals (https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/speech/quarles20191101a.htm). AI could improve price discovery by forecasting demand and supply; it could also undermine it if dominant platforms manipulate attention, personalize prices, or coordinate algorithmic pricing. Property rights are strained because AI models are built on data, copyrighted works, user behavior, compute clusters, and model weights whose ownership boundaries are contested; this is why “data as labor” advocates argue that free user data creates unequal distribution and monopsony-like bargaining problems (https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pandp.20181003). Competition is threatened where frontier AI has scale economies in compute, data, talent, distribution, and cloud access; it is strengthened where cheap AI lets small firms code, market, translate, design, and administer at lower fixed cost (https://doi.org/10.1787/6f88a1ea-en; https://oecd.ai/en/ai-publications/the-effects-of-generative-ai-on-productivity-innovation-and-entrepreneurship).

The strongest post-capitalist case says AI attacks capitalism’s historic bargain: most people accept private ownership because wages give them access to the social product. If machines can produce most goods and services while capital owners control the machines, the system can generate output without distributing enough income to non-owners. Paul Mason’s postcapitalism thesis argues that information technology creates “zero marginal cost,” delinks work from wages, strengthens network effects, and requires breaking tech monopolies, UBI/basic services, and data democracy (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-923X.12851). The Marxian version reaches back to the “Fragment on Machines”: when social knowledge is objectified in machinery as fixed capital, direct labor time becomes less adequate as the measure and basis of value; modern scholars debate whether this really implies collapse, but it remains the strongest theoretical lineage for the claim that AI makes wage-centered capitalism obsolete (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03085147.2017.1397360).

The demand-collapse argument is older than AI. Keynes’s “effective demand” principle rejected the view that supply automatically creates full-employment demand, arguing that output and employment can settle below full employment when expected demand is inadequate (https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks03/0300071h/chap03.html). Underconsumptionists such as J.A. Hobson argued that unequal income distribution can cause excessive saving, insufficient consumption, and depression dynamics (https://www.marxists.org/archive/hobson/1933/11/underconsum.htm). The AI version is: if labor income collapses while output capacity rises, ordinary consumers cannot buy the output; unless capital owners, governments, or new ownership mechanisms recycle income, firms face deficient demand. The best modern formalization is Korinek’s AGI scenario: output rises sharply when labor ceases to constrain production, but competitive wages eventually “plummet” as machines substitute for scarce labor; shared prosperity then requires institutions that compensate workers for lost labor income (https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/scenario-planning-for-an-agi-future-anton-korinek).

The strongest rebuttal is not “nothing changes.” It is that capitalism has multiple demand channels. Capital owners consume and invest; falling prices raise real purchasing power; new goods create new wants; and automation historically has created complementary jobs rather than permanent demand collapse. Autor’s canonical review says automation substitutes for labor but also complements labor, raises output, and increases labor demand in other tasks; he argues commentators often miss these complementarities (https://doi.org/10.1257/JEP.29.3.3). Bessen’s historical work on textiles, steel, and autos finds automation can increase employment when demand is elastic and reduce it when demand becomes satiated, meaning the key variable is demand elasticity, not automation alone (https://www.nber.org/papers/w24235; https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2935003). Say’s-law-style optimists add that income generated in production accrues somewhere, and if savings are invested, demand reappears as investment demand. Keynesians respond that “somewhere” matters: if income accrues to high-saving owners or foreign suppliers, full-employment demand is not automatic.

Concentration is the clearest current stress point. Frontier AI markets are not yet a single monopoly, but the stack is concentrated. Menlo Ventures estimated 2025 U.S. enterprise foundation-model API usage/spend at Anthropic 40%, OpenAI 27%, Google 21%, with the top three at 88%; Menlo notes these are estimates from survey-weighted production usage and public financial triangulation, not audited public market shares (https://menlovc.com/perspective/2025-the-state-of-generative-ai-in-the-enterprise/). Cloud infrastructure is concentrated: Synergy Research reported Q4 2025 worldwide cloud infrastructure shares of Amazon 28%, Microsoft 21%, and Google 14%, with 2025 full-year cloud infrastructure spending at $419 billion (https://www.srgresearch.com/articles/genai-helps-drive-quarterly-cloud-revenues-to-119-billion-as-growth-rate-jumped-yet-again-in-q4). AI chips are more concentrated: OECD reports recent estimates giving Nvidia over 80% of GPU chips used for AI, while Data Center Dynamics, citing TechInsights, reported Nvidia shipped 3.76 million data-center GPUs in 2023 for 98% of data-center GPU shipments (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/competition-in-artificial-intelligence-infrastructure_623d1874-en/full-report/component-5.html; https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/nvidia-gpu-shipments-totaled-376m-in-2023-equating-to-a-98-market-share-report/). TechInsights also estimated the 2023 data-center AI chip market at $17.7 billion, with Nvidia at 65%, Intel 22%, and AMD 11% when broader AI chips are counted (https://www.techinsights.com/blog/data-center-ai-chip-market-q1-2024-update).

The antitrust concern is that compute, data, cloud credits, engineering talent, distribution, and model access may become bottleneck inputs. The FTC’s 2025 report on Microsoft/OpenAI, Amazon/Anthropic, and Alphabet/Anthropic said these partnerships can affect access to compute and talent, increase switching costs, and give cloud partners sensitive technical and business information (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/ftc-issues-staff-report-ai-partnerships-investments-study). The UK CMA similarly warned of risks to fair, open, effective competition in foundation models (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-foundation-models-update-paper). Korinek and Vipra argue foundation models have economies of scale/scope and risks of tipping and vertical integration (https://www.nber.org/papers/w33139). The counter is that existing antitrust law is deliberately general: the FTC says the Sherman Act, Clayton Act, and FTC Act have been applied from “horse and buggies” to digital markets, and Clayton Act Section 7 already reaches mergers whose effect “may be substantially to lessen competition” (https://www.ftc.gov/advice-guidance/competition-guidance/guide-antitrust-laws/antitrust-laws). The market-optimist adds that open models, model routing, synthetic data, cheaper inference, and AI-assisted entrepreneurship lower entry barriers downstream; OECD’s 2026 microdata review finds non-generative AI adoption is not associated with significant increases in market power and that GenAI exposure creates opportunities for smaller firms as well as advantages for incumbents (https://doi.org/10.1787/6f88a1ea-en).

Capital-labor share data make the concern concrete. U.S. nonfarm business labor share, indexed to 2017=100, was 112.828 in Q1 2000, 99.721 in Q1 2017, 97.871 in Q1 2025, 94.843 in Q1 2026, and 93.547 in Q2 2026 in the latest FRED/BLS table (https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/PRS85006173). Karabarbounis and Neiman documented a global decline in labor share since the early 1980s and argued that falling investment-goods prices induced substitution from labor toward capital, explaining roughly half the decline (https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjt032). Their own later NBER work emphasizes measurement disputes between gross and net labor shares, depreciation, and whether factor shares proxy inequality during transitions (https://www.nber.org/papers/w20606). Critics argue measurement, offshoring, demographics, superstar firms, market power, and elasticities of substitution all matter; one review notes Elsby-Hobijn-Sahin’s mismeasurement/offshoring hypothesis, Autor et al.’s superstar-firm hypothesis, and Glover-Short demographics (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057198/). Piketty’s r>g thesis says wealth concentration tends to rise when returns on capital exceed growth, but Acemoglu and Robinson argue “general laws” like r>g ignore institutions and do not explain historical top-income patterns well (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.p20151060; https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/abs/10.1257/jep.29.1.3). AI plausibly pushes capital share up if models and robots substitute for labor and if ownership is concentrated; it can push labor share up in sectors where AI complements scarce workers, raises productivity, and expands demand.

The post-scarcity argument says AI turns production into a solved problem and leaves distribution as politics. Korinek’s AGI scenarios explicitly separate output boom from wage collapse, implying abundance plus concentrated claims unless redistribution changes (https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/scenario-planning-for-an-agi-future-anton-korinek). The counter is that scarcity migrates: energy, chips, data centers, land, water, scarce minerals, trusted human services, status goods, safe neighborhoods, political influence, and human attention remain rival. The IMF’s 2026 scenario exercise flags energy, data centers, and connectivity as diffusion bottlenecks and says labor-tax bases may erode under rapid AI diffusion (https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/068/2026/002/article-A001-en.xml). So “post-scarcity” is best understood as lower marginal cost for many cognitive goods, not the end of scarcity.

Proposed alternatives vary in how much capitalism they preserve. Sovereign wealth/citizen dividends socialize ownership of capital rents while leaving markets intact: Alaska’s 2024 Permanent Fund Dividend was $1,702 per person (https://pfd.alaska.gov/payments/tax-information), and Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global ended 2025 at NOK 21,268 billion with a 15.1% 2025 return (https://www.nbim.no/en/news-and-insights/the-press/press-releases/2026/strong-return-in-2025/). UBI replaces labor income more directly; a large U.S. randomized guaranteed-income study gave 1,000 low-income adults $1,000/month for three years and found non-transfer income fell about $1,800/year, labor-force participation fell 4.1 percentage points, and hours fell 1-2 per week (https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719). That is evidence UBI can support choice but also reduces labor supply somewhat at tested levels. Data dividends treat user data as compensated labor or collective property, but implementation requires valuation, bargaining institutions, privacy safeguards, and likely data unions (https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/pandp.20181003). Worker ownership/co-ops distribute capital income inside firms; Mondragon reported over €11.2 billion in 2024 sales, more than 70,000 employees, and €632 million in profits, showing scale is possible but not proving economy-wide feasibility (https://www.mondragon-corporation.com/en/anual-report/letters-for-the-president/). Compute governance and public AI options would treat compute/model access as infrastructure: public clouds, subsidized compute for universities/SMEs, open public models, and interoperability rules. The IMF explicitly mentions shared infrastructure and public-good AI models as ways to ease diffusion frictions (https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/068/2026/002/article-A001-en.xml).

The “capitalism has survived everything” case is strong. Mechanization, electrification, internal combustion, computers, and the internet all disrupted occupations without ending markets. Crafts finds steam, electricity, and ICT had large productivity effects but often with long lags, and the First Industrial Revolution is not a template for permanently adverse worker living standards (https://doi.org/10.1093/oxrep/grab012). Autor’s central point is that centuries of automation have not eliminated work because technology also creates complements and new demands (https://doi.org/10.1257/JEP.29.3.3). Institutions adapted: the Sherman Act arrived in 1890, the FTC Act and Clayton Act in 1914 (https://www.ftc.gov/advice-guidance/competition-guidance/guide-antitrust-laws/antitrust-laws); the National Labor Relations Act in 1935 created the NLRB and private-sector collective-bargaining rights (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/national-labor-relations-act); Social Security in 1935 established old-age benefits and unemployment insurance architecture (https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/social-security-act); and the FLSA established minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and child-labor rules (https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/compliance-assistance/handy-reference-guide-flsa). In this view, adaptation is survival: a capitalist economy with aggressive antitrust, AI taxes, citizen dividends, public AI, and UBI is still recognizably capitalist if private firms, markets, and profit remain central.

The strongest conclusion is conditional. Advanced AI does not logically require abolishing capitalism. It does plausibly require restructuring if labor ceases to be the main purchasing-power channel or if AI rents concentrate into a compute-cloud-model oligopoly. Market capitalism survives in recognizable form if AI mostly complements labor, lowers entry barriers, and is governed by competition policy plus redistribution. It becomes post-capitalist, or at least no longer wage-centered capitalism, if most value is produced by privately owned autonomous capital while most people lack labor income and meaningful capital claims.

**Disagreement Propositions**

1. **EMPIRICAL:** AI is already reducing labor’s income share materially.  
Resolution: sector-level studies show AI adoption causally lowers wage bills/labor share after controlling for demand, offshoring, firm size, and macro cycles.

2. **PREDICTIVE:** Frontier AI will substitute for most economically valuable labor.  
Resolution: benchmark plus labor-market evidence shows AI systems can perform most tasks at lower total cost than humans across several years of deployment.

3. **EMPIRICAL:** AI markets will tip into durable oligopoly.  
Resolution: five-year evidence on model, cloud, chip, and application shares shows persistent high concentration, high switching costs, and exclusionary input control.

4. **PREDICTIVE:** Demand will collapse if labor income falls.  
Resolution: observe whether transfers, capital income, price declines, new goods, and investment demand offset wage-bill decline without chronic underemployment.

5. **NORMATIVE:** Citizens deserve claims on AI rents because AI is trained on collective human knowledge.  
Resolution: cannot be settled empirically; requires a political theory of ownership, desert, privacy, and public goods.

6. **DEFINITIONAL:** A high-tax, UBI-funded, public-compute economy is still capitalism.  
Resolution: define threshold criteria: private ownership, market allocation, wage centrality, profit motive, and share of output produced by public/nonprofit systems.
