**Research Report**

**Question:** What does the empirical record through mid-2026 show about AI’s effect on employment?

The short answer is: the aggregate U.S. labor market does not yet show a clean “AI unemployment shock,” but several narrower labor-market cells now show patterns that are difficult to dismiss. The strongest displacement case is concentrated in early-career, AI-exposed white-collar work and in online/freelance markets for writing, translation, design, and customer support. The strongest skeptic case is that overall employment remains high, layoffs remain low by historical standards, many cited studies measure productivity rather than headcount, and much of the junior-hiring collapse overlaps with high interest rates, the post-2021 tech correction, offshoring, and ordinary cost-cutting.

**1. Aggregate Data: No Clear Macro Signal Yet**

At the macro level, the U.S. labor market from 2022 through mid-2026 does not look like one in which AI has already produced mass unemployment. The unemployment rate was 3.5% in December 2022, 3.7% in December 2023, 4.1% in December 2024, 4.4% in December 2025, and 4.2% in June 2026, still low by postwar standards despite a cooling labor market (https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_01052024.htm; https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_01102025.htm; https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_01092026.htm; https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm). Labor-force participation was 62.3% in December 2022, 62.5% in December 2023, 62.5% in December 2024, 62.4% in December 2025, and 61.5% in June 2026, a decline that could reflect demographics, immigration, weak hiring, or discouragement rather than AI specifically (https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CIVPART; https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/empsit_01092026.htm).

Productivity is more ambiguous. Nonfarm business labor productivity fell 1.9% in 2022, rose 1.2% in 2023, rose 3.0% in 2024, rose 2.1% in 2025, and was up 2.2% year over year in 2026 Q2 (https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/prod2_02012024.htm; https://www.bls.gov/news.release/prod2.t02.htm). The “nothing visible yet” reading is straightforward: if generative AI were already replacing labor at scale, one would expect a sharper rise in unemployment, broader payroll contraction, or an unmistakable productivity boom. None is visible in aggregate BLS data through mid-2026.

The pro-displacement reading is that macro data are too blunt. AI first hits narrow cells: junior software developers, customer support workers, translators, copywriters, designers, paralegals, and entry-level analysts. Aggregate unemployment can stay low if displaced workers move elsewhere, if firms reduce hiring rather than fire workers, or if AI raises output while suppressing headcount growth in exposed occupations.

**2. Entry-Level White-Collar Hiring**

This is where the empirical debate became serious in 2025-2026.

The strongest evidence for early displacement comes from administrative and job-posting data. Brynjolfsson, Chandar, and Chen’s Stanford Digital Economy Lab paper, using ADP payroll data, reports that workers aged 22-25 in the most AI-exposed occupations experienced a 16% relative employment decline after controlling for firm-level shocks; the pattern was concentrated in software development, customer service, and clerical occupations (https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/). A February 2026 follow-up note says interest rates affect employment generally but do not explain the disproportionate decline in entry-level AI-exposed jobs; it also concedes that with the broadest firm-time controls, the timing becomes significant only in 2024, so earlier declines likely include other factors (https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canaries-interest-rates-and-timinga-more-on-recent-drivers-of-employment-changes-for-young-workers). Census researcher Lee Tucker, using Quarterly Workforce Indicators, finds a 12% regression-adjusted employment decline over 10 quarters for early-career workers aged 22-24 in the most AI-exposed industry-state cells after ChatGPT’s release (https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html).

The private labor-market data point the same direction but are less clean causally. SignalFire’s 2026 State of Talent report says tech hiring overall is running at 75% of its pre-pandemic baseline, while new-grad/entry-level hiring is down roughly 65% at Tech Majors and about 76% at early-stage startups versus 2019 (https://www.signalfire.com/blog/signalfire-state-of-talent-report-2026). SignalFire also says engineering has held up better than design, product, and marketing: at Tech Majors, engineering hiring is down 11% from 2019 while overall hiring is down 25%, and software engineers rose from 46% to 55% of Tech Major hiring (https://www.signalfire.com/blog/signalfire-state-of-talent-report-2026). Indeed reports that tech postings were down 36% from early-2020 levels by July 2025, but also says there is no “smoking gun” linking the plunge to AI because nearly half the net decline from the peak occurred before ChatGPT’s public launch (https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/). Indeed separately finds senior and manager-level tech postings were down 19% from pre-pandemic levels in early 2025, while standard and junior tech titles were down 34%, and the share of postings asking for at least five years’ experience rose from 37% to 42% between 2022 Q2 and 2025 Q2 (https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/experience-requirements-have-tightened-amid-the-tech-hiring-freeze/).

The skeptic case is strong. The 2022-2026 period includes the end of zero-interest-rate hiring, a venture-capital reset, tech overhiring reversal, a broad white-collar hiring slowdown, offshoring, and weak quits. LinkedIn’s April 2026 AI Labor Market Update says entry-level hiring is slowing rather than collapsing, and that the slowdown broadly tracks overall hiring; it still notes U.S. entry-level hiring in AI-augmented roles fell 8.9% year over year versus 1.9% overall (https://www.linkedin.com/posts/linkedin-economic-graph_ai-labor-market-update-april-2026-activity-7454884872873209857-ryRi). Strada’s May 2026 survey of nearly 1,500 executives and senior talent leaders finds employers are more likely to say AI will increase than decrease entry-level hiring in 2026, with 2.7 times as many expecting an increase; however, 42% report AI has increased analytical responsibilities for entry-level workers, 41% say it reduced routine administrative tasks, and 33% say it reduced foundational skill-building tasks (https://www.strada.org/news-insights/entry-level-hiring-in-the-ai-era-what-employers-are-thinking-and-doing).

**3. Directly Exposed Sectors**

Translation and localization show both substitution and recomposition. Upwork’s 2024 platform analysis says generative AI reduced low-value writing and translation work but increased high-value translation earnings by about 7%-8%, with new demand in multilingual model training, quality control, and specialized localization (https://www.upwork.com/research/generative-ai-work-value; https://www.upwork.com/research/ai-translation-future). A 2024 Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization paper on online freelancing finds demand for substitutable skill clusters, including writing and translation, fell 20%-50% relative to counterfactual trends after ChatGPT, while complementary AI work grew (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167268124004591). Upwork later reported that translation and localization demand grew 29% in September 2025 as firms sought human oversight of AI “workslop,” reinforcing the “task loss, job redesign” interpretation (https://www.upwork.com/research/monthly-hiring-report-september-2025).

Customer support is the canonical mixed case. Klarna announced in February 2024 that its OpenAI-powered assistant handled 2.3 million conversations in one month, two-thirds of customer-service chats, did the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents, cut average resolution time from 11 minutes to under 2 minutes, reduced repeat inquiries by 25%, and was expected to improve 2024 profit by $40 million (https://www.prnewswire.co.uk/news-releases/klarna-ai-assistant-handles-two-thirds-of-customer-service-chats-in-its-first-month-302072744.html). But the “700 agents” claim was a workload equivalence, not proof that 700 named employees were laid off. In May 2025, Bloomberg reported Klarna’s CEO said the cost-cutting push had gone too far and the firm was recruiting human agents so customers could reach a person (https://news.bloomberglaw.com/artificial-intelligence/klarna-rethinks-ai-cost-cutting-plan-with-call-for-real-people). Forbes reported Klarna’s own response that it was not reversing AI, still used outsourced agents, and was adding a small human-support pilot because overemphasis on cost had reduced quality (https://www.forbes.com/sites/quickerbettertech/2025/05/18/business-tech-news-klarna-reverses-on-ai-says-customers-like-talking-to-people/).

Call-center evidence shows productivity, not necessarily layoffs. Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond’s QJE article studies 5,172 customer-support agents and finds AI assistance increased issues resolved per hour by 15% on average, with the largest gains for less experienced and lower-skilled workers (https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/140/2/889/7990658). That could reduce demand for agents if output demand is fixed, but it could also raise service quality, reduce turnover, and expand service capacity.

Copywriting, content, illustration, and stock art have clearer short-run freelance losses. Hui, Reshef, and Zhou’s Organization Science paper finds freelancers in occupations affected by ChatGPT, DALL-E 2, and Midjourney suffered reductions in employment and earnings on a large online platform (https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/orsc.2023.18441). Brookings’ summary of online labor-market evidence reports exposed freelancers such as copyeditors and proofreaders saw about a 2% decline in new monthly contracts and roughly 5% lower monthly earnings after ChatGPT (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/is-generative-ai-a-job-killer-evidence-from-the-freelance-market/).

Software engineering mostly shows output gains per developer, not proven net headcount cuts. Randomized trials at Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 company covering 4,867 developers find AI coding assistants increased completed tasks by 26.08%, with higher adoption and larger gains among less experienced developers (https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/mnsc.2025.00535). BIS researchers studying Ant Group’s CodeFuse find code output increased by more than 50%, with statistically significant gains mainly among junior staff (https://www.bis.org/publ/work1208.htm). But METR’s 2025 randomized trial of 16 experienced open-source developers on 246 real tasks found AI access increased completion time by 19%, showing that gains are not universal and may depend on task familiarity, codebase complexity, and review burden (https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/11/ai-coding-tools-may-not-speed-up-every-developer-study-shows/). NBER’s 2026 GitHub telemetry study finds autocomplete, interactive agents, and autonomous agents raised commits by 40%, 140%, and 180%, respectively, but the autonomous-agent effect attenuated to 50% for projects and 30% for releases, implying human review and shipping bottlenecks remain binding (https://www.nber.org/papers/w35275).

**4. Firm-Level Experiments and Macro Estimates**

Noy and Zhang’s controlled writing experiment finds ChatGPT substantially improved time and quality on mid-level professional writing tasks, but such experiments do not measure employer demand or equilibrium employment (https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586). Dell’Acqua et al.’s BCG field experiment finds consultants using GPT-4 completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced more than 40% higher-quality results on tasks inside the model’s capability frontier, but performed worse on tasks outside it (https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=64700). These studies prove productivity potential; they do not prove whether firms hire fewer people or expand output.

Acemoglu’s 2024 NBER paper is the central modest-effects case. Using a task-based model and existing exposure/productivity estimates, he estimates AI will raise TFP by no more than 0.66% over 10 years, and likely less than 0.53%, because many exposed tasks are hard to learn, context-dependent, and not cleanly automated (https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487). Critics argue this approach may undercount new tasks, faster-than-expected adoption, model improvement, organizational redesign, and consumer surplus; Bick, Blandin, and Deming show generative AI adoption was already rapid by late 2024, with 23% of employed respondents using it at work in the prior week and reported time savings equal to 1.4% of total work hours (https://www.nber.org/papers/w32966). Hampole et al. offer a bridge between the two views: task-level AI exposure reduces labor demand, but overall employment effects are modest because AI-adopting firms also expand and reallocate labor (https://www.nber.org/papers/w33509).

**5. AI-Attributed Layoffs**

Challenger, Gray & Christmas data show AI is now a prominent stated reason for layoffs, but these are employer-announced reasons, not audited causal estimates. Challenger says companies cited AI for 54,836 announced layoffs in 2025, 5% of all cuts that year (https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-march-cuts-rise-25-from-february-ai-leads-reasons/). Through June 2026, Challenger says AI was cited in 101,743 cuts, about 23% of all announced cuts, and since 2023 AI had been cited in 173,568 cuts (https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-june-layoffs-cool-to-45849-down-53-from-may-ai-leads-reasons-for-fourth-consecutive-month/). I could not verify a primary Challenger page giving a standalone 2024 AI-attributed total; therefore the exact 2024 number is UNVERIFIED. A conservative verified statement is that 2025 plus 2026-through-June AI-attributed cuts equal 156,579, with some additional but not separately verified 2024 count inside Challenger’s 173,568 since-2023 cumulative figure.

The critique is that “AI” can be cover for cost-cutting. Challenger itself reported that 2026 cuts remained concentrated in technology and restructuring, and other reported reasons included market/economic conditions, closings, contract loss, and restructuring (https://www.challengergray.com/blog/challenger-report-june-layoffs-cool-to-45849-down-53-from-may-ai-leads-reasons-for-fourth-consecutive-month/). Indeed’s tech-postings work similarly warns that the tech hiring freeze began before ChatGPT and tracks broader labor-market weakness (https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/).

**6. Wages Versus Employment**

Wage evidence is less clearly displacement-oriented than employment evidence. Pew, using occupational AI exposure measures, finds workers in the most AI-exposed jobs earned $33 per hour in 2022 versus $20 in the least-exposed jobs, meaning exposure is concentrated in higher-paid, more educated work (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/07/26/earnings-of-workers-with-more-or-less-exposure-to-ai/). Felten, Raj, and Seamans’ exposure work underlies much of this literature and shows occupational heterogeneity in exposure to generative AI rather than direct realized job loss (https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.4414065). Dallas Fed analysis finds a one-standard-deviation increase in AI exposure has little effect on wage growth for occupations with median experience premia, but is associated with a 0.28 percentage-point reduction in wage growth for occupations with zero experience premium, suggesting AI may compress wages where experience does not protect workers (https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0224). Jiang et al. find higher AI exposure is associated with longer workdays and reduced leisure, implying some AI gains may be captured as work intensification rather than higher pay or unemployment (https://www.nber.org/papers/w33536).

**Neutral Disagreement Propositions**

1. **Is the early-career decline causal AI displacement or a correlated tech-cycle shock?**  
Resolving evidence: linked employer-worker data with firm-time controls, AI adoption timing, occupation-level task automation measures, and worker reallocation outcomes.

2. **Do AI productivity gains translate into fewer workers or more output?**  
Resolving evidence: firm-level panels measuring AI adoption, output, prices, headcount, hiring, and task mix over multiple years.

3. **Are junior roles disappearing or being redesigned into higher-judgment roles?**  
Resolving evidence: longitudinal job-posting content, hiring cohorts, internal promotion data, and training investments by occupation.

4. **Are AI-attributed layoffs genuine automation or narrative cover for restructuring?**  
Resolving evidence: audited layoff datasets matching eliminated roles to deployed AI systems, budget reallocations, and post-layoff output.

5. **Will wage effects dominate employment effects?**  
Resolving evidence: occupation-level wage panels separating exposure, adoption, experience premia, bargaining power, and hours worked.
