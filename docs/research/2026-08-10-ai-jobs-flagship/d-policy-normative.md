**Research Report**

**EMPIRICAL: Large-displacement response tools have a mixed record, not a magic record.** U.S. retraining programs show real but modest results. The national Trade Adjustment Assistance evaluation found that TAA substantially increased receipt of services and training, but labor-market results were weak: in the final follow-up year, TAA participants earned about `$3,300` less than matched comparison workers and worked `33` weeks versus `35` weeks for comparisons (https://www.mathematica.org/projects/trade-adjustment-assistance-evaluation). The Department of Labor’s CLEAR review rated the TAA evidence “moderate” and summarized effects as favorable for education/skills but unfavorable for earnings, wages, and employment (https://beta.dol.gov/research-data/clear/studies/estimated-impacts-participants-trade-adjustment-assistance-taa-program-under-2002-amendments). The strongest skeptical interpretation is that retraining often arrives after a worker has already lost firm-specific skills, social networks, seniority, and local opportunity; it cannot by itself rebuild labor demand.

**EMPIRICAL: The countercase is that failures may reflect design and scale, not impossibility.** The WIA/WIOA “Gold Standard” randomized evaluation enrolled more than `34,000` job seekers in `28` local workforce areas and found that staff-assisted intensive services increased earnings by about `$3,300` to `$7,100` over the follow-up period, or `7%` to `20%`, depending on data source; evidence on training itself was inconclusive and suggested no positive impacts within `30` months (https://www.mathematica.org/projects/wia-gold-standard-evaluation). A DOL evidence scan says case management can improve employment and earnings and that work-based and sector-based training look more promising than generic training (https://www.dol.gov/resource-library/research-evidence-scan-key-strategies-related-workforce-innovation-and-opportunity). Card, Kluve, and Weber’s meta-analysis of over `200` ALMP studies found average effects close to zero in the short run but more positive after `2-3` years, with larger gains for human-capital programs and stronger results in recessions (https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jeurec/v16y2018i3p894-931..html). So the strongest pro-retraining claim is not “ordinary retraining works well”; it is “well-funded, targeted, employer-linked, long-horizon programs can help some workers, especially when paired with job creation or demand.”

**EMPIRICAL: UBI evidence is useful but bounded.** Finland’s 2017-2018 basic income experiment randomly selected `2,000` unemployed people aged `25-58` to receive `EUR560` monthly for two years; employment effects were small, with year-two employment about `6` days higher than controls, while life satisfaction, mental wellbeing, and perceived economic security improved (https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-//1271139/perustulokokeilun-tulokset-tyollisyysvaikutukset-vahaisia-toimeentulo-ja-psyykkinen-terveys-koettiin-paremmaksi). Stockton SEED gave `125` residents `$500/month` for `24` months; first-year results showed full-time employment rising from `28%` to `40%` among recipients versus `32%` to `37%` in the control group, with spending concentrated on basics: food `37%`, merchandise `22%`, utilities `11%`, auto costs `10%`, and less than `1%` on alcohol/tobacco (https://www.stocktondemonstration.org/press-landing/guaranteed-income-increases-employment-improves-financial-and-physical-health; https://www.stocktondemonstration.org/employment). The OpenResearch U.S. RCT gave `1,000` low-income adults `$1,000/month` for three years while `2,000` controls received `$50/month`; the public summary reports recipients were `2` percentage points less likely to be employed and worked `1.3` fewer hours per week, while the NBER revision reports a `4.1` percentage-point decrease in labor-market participation and `1-2` fewer work hours per week (https://www.openresearchlab.org/projects/unconditional-cash-study; https://www.nber.org/papers/w32719). OpenResearch found monthly spending rose by about `$310`, mainly food `$67`, rent `$52`, transportation `$50`, and support to others `$22`, a `26%` increase relative to controls (https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/key-findings-spending).

**EMPIRICAL: What UBI experiments cannot show is AI-scale macro adaptation.** These trials tested modest-to-moderate transfers in labor markets where jobs still existed. Finland sampled unemployed benefit recipients, Stockton was small and city-level, and OpenResearch began during COVID disruption but still in a broader economy where employment rates rose for both treatment and control groups (https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/key-findings-employment-and-income). They can inform labor-supply, spending, wellbeing, and agency effects. They cannot prove whether a national UBI would be fiscally sustainable, politically stable, inflation-neutral, or psychologically adequate if AI caused broad labor-demand collapse.

**EMPIRICAL: Distributional exposure is uneven.** Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin, and Rock’s early “GPTs are GPTs” estimate found roughly `80%` of U.S. workers could have at least `10%` of tasks affected and `19%` could have at least `50%` affected; the published Science version estimates only `1.8%` of jobs have over half their tasks exposed with simple LLM interfaces, rising to just over `46%` when likely complementary software is included (https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2303.10130.html; https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adj0998). Felten, Raj, and Seamans find generative-AI exposure is highest in educated, high-paid, white-collar occupations, not only in low-wage routine work (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4414065). Pew’s O*NET analysis found `19%` of U.S. workers in the most AI-exposed jobs and `23%` in the least exposed jobs; Hispanic workers were `13%` most exposed and `34%` least exposed, while foreign-born workers were `15%` most exposed and `31%` least exposed (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/07/26/exposure-of-workers-to-ai/). Exposure is not displacement, but it identifies where bargaining pressure, task redesign, and wage effects may arrive first.

**EMPIRICAL: If capital captures most AI gains, inequality risk rises.** Korinek and Stiglitz argue that worker-replacing AI affects inequality through innovator surplus and factor-price redistribution, and that non-distortionary or well-designed taxation could in principle compensate losers (https://www.nber.org/papers/w24174). Later work emphasizes that labor-saving AI may create winner-take-all dynamics and concentrate gains where capital, data, compute, and model ownership are located (https://www.nber.org/papers/w28453). Korinek’s 2024 policy paper frames transformative AI as potentially reducing labor’s role as a bottleneck factor, making income distribution less reliably tied to wages (https://www.nber.org/papers/w32980). The strongest pro-AI reply is that productivity gains enlarge the pie and can finance compensation; the strongest skeptical reply is that compensation is a political achievement, not an automatic market outcome.

**EMPIRICAL: Regional concentration matters because local shocks do not heal quickly.** The China-shock literature is the best U.S. analogue for geographically concentrated displacement. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson found rising Chinese import competition caused higher unemployment, lower labor-force participation, lower wages, and sharply higher transfer payments in exposed local labor markets; they estimate import competition explained one-quarter of the contemporaneous decline in U.S. manufacturing employment (https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Faer.103.6.2121). Their later synthesis says wages and labor-force participation remained depressed and unemployment elevated for at least a decade in exposed places (https://www.nber.org/papers/w21906). Politically, Autor, Dorn, Hanson, and Majlesi find trade-exposed districts became more polarized, with moderate representatives losing ground and affected counties shifting in different ideological directions depending on initial demographics (https://www.nber.org/papers/w22637). The AI analogy is empirical but not settled: AI exposure is more white-collar and geographically different than manufacturing import exposure, yet the China shock shows why “national net gains” can coexist with concentrated local harm.

**EMPIRICAL: Fiscal institutions are also exposed to labor displacement.** Social Security is largely pay-as-you-go and financed mainly through payroll taxes: SSA reports 2024 OASI/DI revenues were `91.2%` from payroll tax contributions and reimbursements, with only `3.9%` from taxation of benefits and `4.9%` from interest (https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/chartbooks/fast_facts/2025/fast_facts25.html). CBO says payroll taxes account for about `96%` of Social Security revenues under its measure (https://www.cbo.gov/publication/60679). If AI reduces taxable labor income without a replacement tax base, retirement finance becomes harder; if AI instead raises wages or employment in new sectors, the effect could be neutral or positive.

**EMPIRICAL: The “who owns the models” question is not abstract.** Foundation-model markets have scale economies because training costs, data, compute, talent, and distribution are concentrated. Korinek and Vipra argue these features create risks of market tipping and vertical integration, potentially translating AI concentration into broader economic power (https://www.nber.org/papers/w33139). The FTC has warned that compute, data, talent, network effects, and cloud lock-in can create barriers to entry in generative AI markets (https://www.ftc.gov/policy/advocacy-research/tech-at-ftc/2023/06/generative-ai-raises-competition-concerns). The distributional significance is direct: if displaced workers do not own claims on AI capital, productivity gains may show up as asset income rather than wages.

**NORMATIVE: Work as intrinsic value vs work as income mechanism.** One side says work provides meaning, time structure, social contact, status, collective purpose, and activity; Jahoda’s latent-deprivation model and later meta-analysis support that unemployed people are deprived of these functions and that deprivation is associated with worse wellbeing (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10017486/). The other side says those goods need not be tied to paid employment; if AI makes work unnecessary, policy should distribute income and build alternative institutions for purpose, care, learning, and civic contribution. Evidence can measure mental health, social isolation, identity, and life satisfaction. Evidence cannot settle whether a life without paid work is dignified liberation or social catastrophe.

**NORMATIVE: Who owes transition costs?** The polluter-pays analogy says AI developers and deployers who profit from displacement owe wage insurance, retraining, severance, relocation, or public contributions because they impose adjustment costs on identifiable workers. The market-process view says productivity shocks are ordinary competition; firms owe compliance with labor law, while redistribution belongs to the general tax-and-transfer system. Evidence can estimate displacement, profits, incidence, and feasible taxes. It cannot settle the moral boundary between innovation and compensable harm.

**NORMATIVE: Should deployment slow to match adaptation speed?** The “speed limit” position says society should pace AI adoption so training, bargaining, tax reform, and regional adjustment can catch up. The counterposition says slowing beneficial technology sacrifices growth, medical discovery, safety improvements, accessibility, and consumer surplus; some forgone benefits may cost lives or welfare, even if hard to attribute ex ante. Evidence can estimate adoption rates, displacement, productivity, and sectoral benefits. It cannot determine the acceptable tradeoff between present workers’ security and future beneficiaries’ gains.

**PROCEDURAL: Who decides deployment terms?** Labor-side governance says workers should bargain over AI use before jobs are redesigned. Live precedents include WGA’s 2023 MBA provisions stating AI is not a writer, AI material cannot reduce writers’ credit or compensation, companies cannot require writers to use AI, and companies must disclose AI-generated material (https://www.wga.org/contracts/know-your-rights/artificial-intelligence). SAG-AFTRA’s 2023 provisions require informed consent and compensation for digital replicas in most cases (https://www.sagaftra.org/contracts-industry-resources/contracts/2023-tvtheatrical-contracts/artificial-intelligence-resources). Dockworkers’ automation fight shows the harder industrial version: the ILA’s 2025 agreement emphasized wage gains and automation protections after a 2024 strike (https://ilaunion.org/international-longshoremens-association-president-harold-j-daggett-leads-his-rank-and-file-members-to-landmark-tentative-agreement-on-ila-master-contract/). Management prerogative says firms must retain authority to adopt productivity-enhancing tools, subject to law and contract. Evidence can compare productivity, wages, employment, and safety under different governance regimes. It cannot settle how much veto power labor should have over capital investment.

**EMPIRICAL: The middle path is displacement without catastrophe, but it requires institutions.** Wage insurance has evidence: a 2024 TAA-linked study summarized by DOL reports wage-insurance eligibility increased short-run employment and long-run cumulative earnings, mainly by shortening non-employment durations, and could be self-financing under conservative assumptions (https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/tradeact/reports/research?lang=en). Sectoral bargaining can compress wage dispersion; OECD finds wage dispersion is smallest among workers covered by sectoral bargaining and coordinated systems are associated with higher employment and lower unemployment than fully decentralized systems (https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/negotiating-our-way-up_1fd2da34-en/full-report/component-6.html). Work-hour reduction is historically real: the U.S. workweek fell from about `60` hours in 1900 to `40` by 1950, with one study attributing much of the change to rising wages and cheaper leisure (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165188908000997). But the Fair Labor Standards Act evidence is mixed: overtime rules reduced long hours, yet did not clearly increase employment in retail/wholesale trade (https://www.nber.org/papers/w6855). Finally, Baumol-style service demand may absorb labor into care, education, health, craft, hospitality, and human services because productivity growth is slower and income demand can rise, but those jobs may be low-wage unless bargaining, public funding, or norms improve (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0954349X05000469).

**Open Disagreement Propositions**

1. **EMPIRICAL:** AI displacement will be geographically and occupationally concentrated enough to reproduce China-shock-style political damage. Evidence can test exposure, layoffs, wages, migration, and voting patterns; it cannot yet prove the analogy because AI’s occupational footprint differs from trade.

2. **EMPIRICAL:** Scaled retraining plus wage insurance can handle large AI displacement. Evidence can compare program designs, earnings, reemployment, and fiscal costs; it cannot assume current modest programs represent what a national emergency-scale system would do.

3. **NORMATIVE:** A society with high UBI and much less paid work is acceptable. Evidence can measure wellbeing, health, family stability, social trust, and civic participation; it cannot settle whether paid work is intrinsically necessary for dignity.

4. **NORMATIVE:** AI firms owe special compensation to displaced workers. Evidence can estimate causal harm and capacity to pay; it cannot determine whether displacement is a compensable externality or ordinary market change.

5. **PROCEDURAL:** Workers should have bargaining or veto rights over AI deployment. Evidence can inform productivity, safety, wages, and job quality under worker voice; it cannot settle who legitimately controls the pace and terms of technological change.
