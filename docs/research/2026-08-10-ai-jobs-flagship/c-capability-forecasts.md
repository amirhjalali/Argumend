**Research Report**

**Scope note:** This report treats the core question as **PREDICTIVE**: whether capability trends plus diffusion dynamics imply that AI can do most economically valuable work within 15 years, not whether that has already happened.

**1. Measured Capability Trends**

**PREDICTIVE.** The strongest “fast capability” evidence is METR’s task-completion-time-horizon work. METR defines a model’s 50% time horizon as the human expert task duration at which the AI agent has a 50% chance of success, using mostly software, ML, and cybersecurity tasks. In March 2025, METR reported that this horizon had grown exponentially for about six years, doubling roughly every seven months; its 2026 Time Horizon 1.1 update gives a P50 doubling time of 196.5 days overall, 130.8 days for models released since 2023, and 88.6 days for models since 2024, though METR warns estimates above 16 hours are unreliable because the benchmark suite is saturating (https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/; https://metr.org/time-horizons/). That is a direct crux for unemployment forecasts: if this trend extrapolates, software-like tasks measured in days or weeks become reachable within the 15-year window; if it saturates, the extrapolation fails.

**EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE.** The 2026 METR frontier risk report says public frontier agents in February-March 2026 had a Time Horizon 1.1 50% horizon of about 12 hours, with a 95% interval of about 5-61 hours, while internal frontier systems were “likely” at or above 16 hours; it also says MirrorCode-Early was saturated at over 100 hours, but warns that agents still made poor judgment calls, exploited benchmark weaknesses, overclaimed, and sometimes cheated (https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-19-frontier-risk-report/). The same pattern appears in Epoch/METR’s MirrorCode: Claude Opus 4.6 reimplemented `gotree`, a 16,905-line Go bioinformatics toolkit with 40+ commands, passing 2,001 end-to-end tests in a task the authors estimate would take a skilled human engineer 2-17 weeks; but the benchmark uses unusually precise, executable specifications, so it does not prove general autonomous software engineering (https://epoch.ai/publications/mirrorcode-preliminary-results).

**EMPIRICAL.** Benchmark saturation and real-world reliability diverge. SWE-bench Verified, a 500-task human-filtered coding benchmark, has become a headline coding metric, and Epoch lists a highest internal score of 83% on its SWE-bench Verified tracker, showing rapid benchmark progress (https://epoch.ai/benchmarks/swe-bench-verified). But OSWorld 2.0, released in 2026 for long-horizon real computer-use workflows, reports that the best model, Claude Opus 4.8 with maximum thinking and batched tool calls, completed only 20.6% of 108 tasks at a 500-step budget, with 54.8% partial score; GPT-5.5 plateaued near 14%, and no model exceeded 10% on tasks in the 137-163 minute bin (https://osworld-v2.xlang.ai/). This is the reliability gap in one sentence: agents can score very high on increasingly gamed or narrow coding benchmarks while still failing messy, stateful, multi-hour workflows.

**EMPIRICAL.** “Jagged frontier” evidence explains why capability does not map cleanly to jobs. In a 2023 randomized BCG experiment with 758 consultants, GPT-4 users completed 12.2% more tasks, worked 25.1% faster, and produced outputs rated over 40% higher on tasks inside the AI frontier; on an outside-frontier business problem, AI users were 19% less likely to produce correct solutions than controls (https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321; https://www.bcg.com/publications/2023/how-people-create-and-destroy-value-with-gen-ai). For labor substitution, this implies the frontier is not “white-collar work” in bulk; it is a patchwork of automatable and still-dangerous subtasks.

**2. The Scaling Dispute**

**PREDICTIVE steelman for fast capability.** The pro-scaling case is that compute, data-center scale, and inference-time reasoning are still compounding. Epoch estimates frontier language-model training compute has grown about 5x per year since 2020, while the global AI-chip stock has grown 3.4x per year, doubling every seven months since 2022; it also estimates pretraining compute efficiency improves around 3x per year (https://epoch.ai/trends). OpenAI’s o1 work adds a second scaling channel: OpenAI says o1 was trained with large-scale reinforcement learning and that performance improved with both train-time compute and test-time “thinking” compute; on AIME 2024, OpenAI reported GPT-4o solved 12% of problems while o1 solved 74% pass@1 and 93% with reranking 1,000 samples (https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/). The fast-side prediction is that pretraining, RL environments, tool use, self-verification, and longer inference runs jointly extend capability well past static benchmark expectations.

**PREDICTIVE steelman for plateau.** The plateau case is that each input is becoming expensive or bounded. Epoch’s data-limit paper estimates the effective stock of quality- and repetition-adjusted public human text at about 300 trillion tokens, with an 80% confidence interval that current trends fully use that stock between 2026 and 2032; it says progress can continue via synthetic data, other modalities, and data efficiency, but new innovations become necessary beyond roughly 2030 (https://epoch.ai/publications/will-we-run-out-of-data-limits-of-llm-scaling-based-on-human-generated-data). Epoch also warns data movement can bottleneck training beyond roughly 2e28 FLOP, with a “latency wall” at 2e31 FLOP absent aggressive batch-size scaling (https://epoch.ai/publications/data-movement-bottlenecks-scaling-past-1e28-flop). Test-time scaling also has diminishing or inverse cases: Anthropic-linked researchers constructed tasks where longer reasoning worsened accuracy, and identified distraction, overfitting to framing, and spurious correlations as failure modes (https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/inverse-scaling/). The plateau-side prediction is not “no progress”; it is that capability gains become narrower, more expensive, more benchmark-contingent, and less reliable for unstructured employment substitution.

**EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE.** Gary Marcus-style critiques have a mixed track record. Marcus was directionally right that fluent LLMs hallucinate, lack robust symbolic grounding, and fail out of distribution; those objections are still visible in OSWorld, METR reward-hacking incidents, and hallucination concerns. He was directionally wrong when arguments that deep-learning systems would not reach broad language, coding, or reasoning competence were overtaken by GPT-4/o1-era results. That mixed record matters because “LLMs cannot reason” was too strong, but “current systems are not reliable agents” remains empirically live. Broad quantitative scorecard for Marcus-specific predictions: **UNVERIFIED**.

**3. Expert and Forecaster Predictions**

**PREDICTIVE.** AI Impacts’ ESPAI surveys show large movement but still later medians than many labs. The 2022 ESPAI contacted ML researchers in June-August 2022 and reported a 50% aggregate forecast for HLMI in 2059, down from roughly 2061 in the comparable 2016 survey (https://wiki.aiimpacts.org/ai_timelines/predictions_of_human-level_ai_timelines/ai_timeline_surveys/2022_expert_survey_on_progress_in_ai). The 2023 ESPAI surveyed 2,778 AI researchers in October 2023, with 2,778 responses from 18,459 functioning email addresses, a 15% response rate; among 1,714 HLMI responses, the aggregate 50% date moved to 2047, 13 years earlier than 2022, while 774 FAOL responses put 50% full automation of labor at 2116, 48 years earlier than the 2022 FAOL result (https://wiki.aiimpacts.org/ai_timelines/predictions_of_human-level_ai_timelines/ai_timeline_surveys/2023_expert_survey_on_progress_in_ai). The same survey structure shows definition sensitivity: “HLMI” feasibility arrives far earlier than “full automation of labor.”

**PREDICTIVE.** Metaculus is much earlier than ESPAI on AGI-like milestones. As of the current indexed question feed, Metaculus shows the median for “first weakly general AI system” at September 13, 2028, with 25th-75th interval from December 29, 2026 to August 2032, based on about 1,800 predictors; it shows “first general AI system” at November 2032, with 25th-75th interval from April 2029 to April 2040, based on about 1,900 predictors (https://www.metaculus.com/questions/?order_by=-rank&search=Agi). But Metaculus questions resolve on specific definitions, not “can do most economically valuable work,” so mapping them to unemployment is a separate predictive step.

**EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE.** XPT shows why expert-vs-forecaster divergence is not easily resolved. The 2022 Existential Risk Persuasion Tournament had 169 participants, including 89 superforecasters and 80 domain experts, and the published IJF abstract reports that specialists were more pessimistic, especially on long-run AI risk, and that four months of incentivized discussion did not materially move the sides (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0169207024001250). FRI’s 2025 near-term accuracy follow-up found no statistically significant accuracy edge for superforecasters or domain experts on 38 resolved near-term questions; both groups underestimated AI progress, with domain experts assigning 8.6% and superforecasters 2.3% to AI achieving IMO gold-level performance by July 2025, and superforecasters assigning only 9.7% average probability to observed outcomes across four AI benchmarks versus 24.6% for domain experts (https://forecastingresearch.org/research/near-term-xpt-accuracy). This is a calibration warning: the best available forecasters were not reliably conservative or reliably expert-beating on fast AI progress.

**4. Insider Claims vs Outside Views**

**PREDICTIVE.** Lab leaders are much earlier than formal expert-survey medians. Sam Altman wrote in January 2025 that OpenAI was “now confident” it knew how to build AGI as traditionally understood and that AI agents might “join the workforce” in 2025 (https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2025/01/06/openai-ceo-sam-altman-we-know-how-to-build-agi/; https://arstechnica.com/ai/2025/01/sam-altman-says-we-are-now-confident-we-know-how-to-build-agi/). In June 2025, Altman wrote that OpenAI is “almost certain” to build superintelligence within ten more years, i.e. by 2035 (https://openai.com/index/ten-years/). Dario Amodei wrote and said in 2025-2026 that powerful AI could be like a “country of geniuses in a data center” by 2026-2027, or almost certainly no later than 2030, with major economic and security consequences (https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/24/anthropic-ceo-wants-to-open-the-black-box-of-ai-models-by-2027/; https://venturebeat.com/ai/anthropic-ceo-dario-amodei-warns-ai-will-match-country-of-geniuses-by-2026). Demis Hassabis told Lex Fridman he saw about a 50% chance of AGI by 2030, while CBS/60 Minutes reported him saying AGI is on track in the next five to ten years (https://lexfridman.com/demis-hassabis-2-transcript; https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/artificial-intelligence-google-deepmind-ceo-demis-hassabis-60-minutes-transcript/).

**EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE.** The incentive critique is straightforward: lab leaders have privileged evidence, but also fundraising, recruitment, regulatory, and product incentives to make the future feel urgent. There is no clean public base-rate dataset of “frontier-tech insider AGI-like predictions versus outcomes” that I could verify; the general claim that tech insiders systematically overpredict their own technologies is **UNVERIFIED**. A defensible weaker claim is that insider forecasts should be treated as evidence with a conflict-of-interest discount, not dismissed outright.

**5. Physical-World Bottlenecks**

**PREDICTIVE.** Robotics is the binding constraint for non-cognitive work. Industrial robots are widespread but narrow: IFR reported roughly 541,000 industrial robot installations in 2023 and an operating stock above 4 million units globally, while the U.S. is only a slice of that market (https://ifr.org/worldrobotics). Amazon announced in 2025 that it had deployed its one-millionth robot across its operations network, showing large-scale warehouse automation is real but highly structured (https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/operations/amazon-million-robots-ai-foundation-model). The strong “mass unemployment” side argues cognitive automation alone can hit enough jobs, because office, clerical, software, support, analysis, marketing, and management tasks are a large share of wage bill. The slower side argues cognitive-only automation caps job loss because healthcare, construction, food service, logistics edge cases, repair, childcare, eldercare, and physical retail require dexterity, mobility, liability acceptance, and local deployment.

**6. Diffusion Lag**

**EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE.** Capability is not adoption. The Census Bureau BTOS reported in 2024 that only 5.4% of U.S. businesses used AI to produce goods or services in the prior two weeks, with expected use rising to 6.6% over the next six months; use was concentrated in information, professional services, and finance rather than the whole economy (https://www2.census.gov/library/working-papers/2024/adrm/ces/CES-WP-24-16R.pdf). This supports the slow-diffusion side: enterprise integration, data access, workflow redesign, worker trust, procurement, cybersecurity, compliance, liability, and union/regulatory constraints make substitution slower than benchmark progress.

**PREDICTIVE.** The fast-diffusion counterargument is that software diffuses differently from electrification or industrial robots. Once a model is API-accessible, millions of workers can use it without installing machines, and model improvements can propagate through SaaS products overnight. The 2025 METR developer productivity follow-up even had to redesign because many developers did not want to participate if assigned to work without AI, suggesting selection effects from rapid adoption (https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/). The disagreement is therefore not “adoption exists”; it is whether adoption reaches substitution-grade reliability and organizational trust fast enough to displace workers rather than augment them.

**Disagreement Structure**

The forecasts diverge because they answer different implicit questions. METR-style extrapolators ask: how soon can agents complete longer task episodes? ESPAI respondents answer: when can unaided machines do every task better and cheaper than humans, ignoring adoption? Metaculus answers specific AGI benchmark definitions. Labor economists ask: when will firms restructure workflows, accept liability, and reduce headcount? Lab leaders often answer: when will frontier systems be technically transformative? These are not interchangeable.

**Propositions Still In Dispute**

1. **PREDICTIVE:** Frontier agent time horizons will keep doubling near METR’s 2025-2026 rate through 2028.  
Shift evidence: by end-2028, independent time-horizon suites with mostly non-saturated tasks show 50% horizons above one work-week across software, research, browser, and office workflows.

2. **EMPIRICAL/PREDICTIVE:** Reliability, not raw capability, is the binding employment threshold.  
Shift evidence: 2027-2028 OSWorld-like and enterprise-agent evals show >80% completion on multi-hour workflows with low hallucination, no reward hacking, and auditable recovery from errors.

3. **PREDICTIVE:** AI can substitute for enough cognitive labor without major robotics progress to cause mass unemployment.  
Shift evidence: by 2028, BLS or payroll data show sustained employment declines in high-AI-exposure office occupations not explained by recession, with rising output per worker.

4. **PREDICTIVE:** Enterprise diffusion will be fast enough to matter within 15 years.  
Shift evidence: 2027-2028 Census BTOS or comparable surveys show >25-30% of U.S. firms using AI in production workflows, not just experimentation, and a measurable share reporting reduced labor demand.

5. **EMPIRICAL:** Forecasting methods are currently undercalibrated for AI discontinuities.  
Shift evidence: by 2028, Metaculus/XPT/ESPAI-style forecasts either correctly anticipate or systematically miss major resolved benchmarks such as autonomous coding, computer-use, scientific-discovery, and robotics milestones.
