**Addendum: Capability/Timeline Symmetry Corrections**

**1. Independent Capability-Skeptic Case**

Narayanan and Kapoor’s “AI as Normal Technology” is not a claim that AI is trivial. It is a claim that even transformative technologies usually become economically powerful through institutions, deployment, complementary capital, regulation, workflow redesign, and diffusion. They explicitly include electricity and the internet as “normal” technologies, while rejecting the frame of AI as a separate autonomous species-like actor (https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology). Their key labor-market implication is that capability is not power: they distinguish “capability and power,” and argue that social impact depends less on an abstract intelligence score than on who can deploy systems reliably into real organizations (https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology). They predict transformative impacts will be “slow (on the timescale of decades)” because AI methods, applications, and adoption move at different speeds (https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology). Their policy point is also diffusion-focused: benefits are not automatic because there are “many roadblocks to AI diffusion” (https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology). The source-hygiene correction is that this is a best-in-class skeptical argument from researchers who built reputational capital by criticizing inflated AI claims before the current boom through AI Snake Oil, their newsletter/book project on misleading AI claims (https://www.aisnakeoil.com/; https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691249131/ai-snake-oil).

Fast-side rebuttal: normal-technology analogies may underweight software’s near-zero marginal replication cost and the possibility that AI automates part of R&D itself. If capability progress continues to compound in agentic software work, diffusion lags could compress relative to electricity-era physical buildout. METR’s time-horizon work is the fast side’s strongest empirical answer: it reports that AI agents’ task-completion horizon on software/ML/cyber tasks has risen rapidly, with the metric defined as the human task duration at which a model is predicted to succeed at a given rate (https://metr.org/time-horizons/).

Melanie Mitchell’s critique is construct validity: benchmark names often outrun what the benchmark actually measures. A benchmark labeled “reasoning,” “planning,” or “abstraction” may measure memorization, prompt sensitivity, contamination, or brittle pattern matching instead. Mitchell’s 2026 AI Magazine article lays out “six principles” for evaluating cognitive capabilities, emphasizing robustness to task variations and mechanisms, not just accuracy (https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/aaai.70061). In The Yale Review she writes that benchmark performance “rarely predicts” real-world capability, highlighting contamination and brittleness as recurring failure modes (https://yalereview.org/article/melanie-mitchell-jagged-intelligence). Her related work on abstraction and analogy argues that AI systems should be tested for robustness and solution strategy, not only headline scores (https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/09637214261433528/).

Fast-side rebuttal: benchmark skepticism is valid, but some benchmarks are increasingly built from private, human-calibrated, tool-using tasks rather than static multiple-choice tests. METR’s HCAST/RE-Bench-derived time-horizon suite uses human completion times and repeated task attempts, which is intended to reduce the gap between benchmark score and economically relevant work (https://metr.org/time-horizons/). This does not eliminate construct-validity concerns; it narrows them.

Subbarao Kambhampati’s planning critique is that LLMs are not reliable autonomous planners merely because they produce plausible plan text. His group’s planning work argues that LLMs can help planning systems but often fail at plan validity, state tracking, and systematic generalization without external verifiers or planners (https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15771; https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01817). The important correction is not “LLMs cannot reason”; it is that valid planning is an exacting computational task, and fluent intermediate reasoning is not evidence of executable, constraint-satisfying plans.

Fast-side rebuttal: tool-using agents can wrap LLMs in search, code execution, verifiers, and external planners. If the economic task is “produce a working patch” rather than “internally solve PDDL,” then hybrid scaffolds may be enough. METR itself evaluates model-plus-scaffold systems, not bare text models, and says elicitation includes tools, command line access, and agent loops (https://metr.org/time-horizons/).

François Chollet’s ARC-AGI case is the cleanest capability-skeptic benchmark argument: ARC-AGI tries to measure skill acquisition and abstraction from very few examples, rather than memorized internet knowledge. ARC Prize’s 2026 ARC-AGI-2 page describes a private evaluation set and an 85% threshold for a bonus prize, with no internet access and open-sourcing requirements (https://arcprize.org/competitions/2026/arc-agi-2). I could verify the 2026 competition structure and prize threshold, but not a live official ARC-AGI-2 frontier leaderboard in the retrieved source; any “latest score” beyond the official competition page is **UNVERIFIED** unless pulled directly from ARC Prize/Kaggle at time of publication. The defensible wording is: ARC-AGI-2 remains a high-salience abstraction benchmark whose unresolved prize threshold indicates that frontier systems have not publicly cleared its intended bar (https://arcprize.org/competitions/2026/arc-agi-2).

Fast-side rebuttal: ARC may be a poor proxy for mass unemployment because the economy rewards useful partial automation, not general abstraction in colored grid puzzles. A model can fail ARC-style few-shot abstraction while still automating coding, customer support, translation, analysis, or clerical workflows. Goldman’s and IMF’s exposure analyses are task/economic analyses, not ARC-style AGI tests (https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity; https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/how-will-ai-affect-the-us-labor-market).

**2. Source Hygiene And Forecast Ecosystem Disclosure**

The disclosure correction is structural, not accusatory. Forecasting and evaluation organizations should receive the same funding/context treatment as lab CEOs.

FRI states that Coefficient Giving, formerly Open Philanthropy, gave it $5,352,800 in December 2022 for general support, $150,000 in March 2023 for AI adversarial collaboration, $60,000 in January 2024 for forecasting-accuracy analysis, $100,000 in April 2024 for ForecastBench, $483,850 in April 2024 for red-line evaluations, $1,071,000 in October 2024 for LEAP, and $10,080,000 in March 2025 for general support (https://forecastingresearch.org/transparency). It also lists $1,300,000 from Vanguard Charitable on behalf of Craig Falls for LEAP and notes some undisclosed research-collaboration funding (https://forecastingresearch.org/transparency).

Epoch AI lists Coefficient/Open Philanthropy support of $1,960,000 in June 2022, $188,558 in February 2023, $6,922,565 in April 2023, $4,132,488 in April 2024, $70,000 in March 2025 for FrontierMath improvements, and $8,500,000 in April 2025 for general support (https://epoch.ai/about/transparency). Epoch also discloses other funders, including Jaan Tallinn at $600,000 and Likith Govindaiah at $400,000 in January 2025, and says it has contractual relationships/collaborations with AI organizations; it specifically says it partnered with OpenAI to create FrontierMath (https://epoch.ai/about/transparency).

AI Impacts reported a $364,893 Open Philanthropy grant for three months of support in 2022 and a $546,000 grant from Jaan Tallinn through the Survival and Flourishing Fund (https://aiimpacts.org/december-2022-updates-and-fundraising/). Good Ventures’ grant database also lists Open Philanthropy Project grants to AI Impacts of $32,000 in 2016, $100,000 in 2018, $50,000 in 2020, and $345,000 in 2023 for the expert survey (https://www.goodventures.org/our-portfolio/grants/ai-impacts-general-support/; https://www.goodventures.org/our-portfolio/grants-database/page/109/?limit=10000&orderby=title&sort=asc).

METR’s public homepage says it has partnered with OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Amazon, and AISI-linked bodies for frontier risk assessments, and that those companies provide access and tokens (https://metr.org/). METR also says it “does not accept compensation for this work” (https://metr.org/). Therefore, “METR has paid evaluation contracts with OpenAI/Anthropic” should be corrected to: METR has evaluation partnerships/access relationships with OpenAI and Anthropic, but public METR materials say no compensation; any private contractual terms are **UNVERIFIED** (https://metr.org/).

Known METR metric critiques should be included in the base corpus. First, P50 and P80 differ sharply. BenchmarkList’s extraction of METR Time Horizon 1.1, sampled June 30, 2026, reports Claude Mythos preview at p50 1044.78 minutes but p80 185.91 minutes; Claude Opus 4.6 at p50 718.81 minutes but p80 69.87 minutes; and GPT-5 at p50 203.01 minutes but p80 38.31 minutes (https://benchmarklist.com/benchmarks/metr_time_horizon_1_1/). Second, task-domain narrowness matters: METR says its tasks primarily consist of software engineering, ML, and cybersecurity, and explicitly warns that an 8-hour horizon does not mean AI can automate all jobs (https://metr.org/time-horizons/). Third, messy-context work is a live limitation: METR says most jobs involve people, ambiguous success metrics, and non-algorithmic evaluation; it also says performance drops when scored holistically rather than algorithmically (https://metr.org/time-horizons/). METR’s own response is that the metric is still useful for trend measurement, but not a direct job-automation forecast (https://metr.org/time-horizons/).

Forecasting base rates also need repair. Armstrong and Sotala’s AI-prediction work found poor reliability and overconfidence in historical AI forecasts; their later paper emphasizes expert overconfidence and the superiority of models over raw expert judgment (https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0952813x.2014.895105). AI Impacts later noted an error in the expert-vs-nonexpert comparison, so the strongest defensible takeaway is not “experts are useless,” but “timeline predictions have large uncertainty, contradictory estimates, and weak historical calibration” (https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/error-in-armstrong-and-sotala-2012; https://wiki.aiimpacts.org/ai_timelines/predictions_of_human-level_ai_timelines/interpretation_of_ai_predictions/accuracy_of_ai_predictions/accuracy_of_ai_predictions).

Autonomous vehicles are the best recent base-rate analogue for embodied/agentic deployment. In 2013, Nissan CEO Carlos Ghosn said Nissan would be ready to introduce Autonomous Drive by 2020 (https://www.industryweek.com/innovation/product-development/article/21961105/nissan-to-offer-self-driving-cars-by-2020). In 2016, Ford CEO Mark Fields said Ford intended high-volume Level 4 vehicles in commercial ride-sharing operation in 2021 (https://media.ford.com/content/fordmedia/feu/gb/en/news/2016/08/16/ford-targets-fully-autonomous-vehicle-for-ride-sharing-in-2021--.html). In 2019, Elon Musk predicted more than one million Tesla robotaxis in 2020 (https://www.cbsnews.com/news/elon-musk-claims-tesla-will-have-self-driving-robotaxis-on-the-road-next-year/). GM Cruise set and then missed a 2019 robotaxi launch target in San Francisco (https://techcrunch.com/2019/07/24/cruise-will-launch-a-commercial-robotaxi-service-in-san-francisco-but-not-in-2019/). Ford later shut down Argo AI after concluding commercial Level 4 scale required more time and capital, taking a $2.7 billion impairment (https://techcrunch.com/2022/10/26/ford-vw-backed-argo-ai-is-shutting-down/; https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/37996/000003799622000073/f-20220930.htm). This does not prove AI labor forecasts are wrong; it supplies a base rate for high-confidence deployment timelines in safety-critical, real-world automation.

**3. Missing Institutional Estimates**

Yale Budget Lab/Brookings-style labor tracking belongs in the corpus because it separates exposure from observed labor-market change. The retrieved summaries of Yale Budget Lab’s 2025/2026 work report no broad, statistically clear AI disruption in occupational mix, employment, wages, or unemployment duration through roughly 33 months after ChatGPT, and compare the AI period with PC-era and internet-era benchmarks (https://parallect.ai/reports/yale-budget-lab-no-ai-labor-disruption-2025-03b3cc; https://www.thetreeline.pub/p/ais-transformation-of-work-june-to). The exact official Yale table values should be pulled from the Budget Lab data repository before publication; third-party summaries conflict on whether a dissimilarity index value is 0.26 or a smaller scaled value, so that figure is **UNVERIFIED**.

IMF Cazzaniga et al. estimate that almost 40% of global employment is AI-exposed; advanced economies are around 60%, emerging markets 40%, and low-income countries 26% (https://www.imf.org/en/Blogs/Articles/2024/01/14/ai-will-transform-the-global-economy-lets-make-sure-it-benefits-humanity). In the staff note, advanced economies split into 27% high-exposure/high-complementarity and 33% high-exposure/low-complementarity; emerging markets split 16% and 24%; low-income countries split 8% and 18% (https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/006/2024/001/article-A001-en.xml). This is not a mass-unemployment prediction; it is an exposure/complementarity map.

Goldman Sachs’ Briggs and Kodnani figure is often misquoted. The claim is that generative AI exposes the equivalent of about 300 million full-time jobs globally to automation, not that 300 million workers will necessarily be unemployed (https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/how-will-ai-affect-the-us-labor-market). Goldman’s later explanation says broad adoption is a roughly 10-year base-case transition, with 6-7% of workers displaced during that period and a 0.6 percentage-point unemployment-rate effect if spread over a decade (https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/how-will-ai-affect-the-us-labor-market). It also emphasizes offsetting labor demand where AI augments workers and infrastructure buildout creates jobs (https://www.goldmansachs.com/insights/articles/the-jobs-ai-is-likely-to-boost-and-those-it-may-disrupt).

Robotics is still mostly pilots, not mass substitution. A July 2026 evidence review says Agility’s Digit has the clearest recurring commercial logistics deployment, including a GXO multi-year operation and more than 100,000 totes moved; Figure reports Figure 02 ran on a BMW line, accumulated more than 1,250 hours, and loaded more than 90,000 parts (https://physicalaiguide.com/guides/humanoid-robots-2026-availability/). These are meaningful pilots, but tiny relative to U.S. warehousing/manufacturing employment. Claims of tens of thousands of deployed humanoids or thousands of Tesla Optimus units in productive factory work should be treated cautiously unless sourced to company filings or audited deployment logs; some tracker claims are **UNVERIFIED** because they aggregate announcements, investor claims, and estimates (https://humanoidintel.ai/humanoid-robots-deployed/). The conceptual reason is Moravec’s paradox: tasks humans find sensorimotor-obvious can be harder for machines than abstract symbolic tasks, a long-standing robotics observation associated with Hans Moravec (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moravec%27s_paradox).

**Closing: Revised Disagreement Propositions**

1. **PREDICTIVE:** By August 11, 2041, will U.S. unemployment exceed 10% for at least six consecutive months primarily because AI systems substitute for human labor? Resolution requires BLS unemployment data plus at least two independent decompositions attributing the rise primarily to AI.

2. **EMPIRICAL:** Through 2031, will AI-exposed occupations show statistically significant employment declines relative to matched low-exposure occupations after controlling for rates, industry cycles, and education? Resolution requires CPS/ADP/BLS microdata and preregistered exposure measures.

3. **PREDICTIVE:** By 2031, will frontier agents reach p80 time horizons above one full workday on messy, multi-party, non-software tasks with human-judged success criteria? Resolution requires an independent benchmark with public protocol and private test tasks.

4. **EMPIRICAL:** Will humanoid/general-purpose robots move from pilots to scale by 2031, defined as 100,000+ audited U.S. deployments performing paid productive work without continuous teleoperation? Resolution requires audited fleet counts, customer sites, and task-hour logs.
