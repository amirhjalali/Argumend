**ADDENDUM: Workers, Distribution, and Geography**

Scope note: this addendum treats “AI unemployment” as an EMPIRICAL labor-market forecast and “adjustment” as partly NORMATIVE: job counts, wages, control, health, and geography can point in different directions.

**1. Who Is Exposed: Gender, Race, Class**

The strongest correction to the earlier corpus is that AI exposure is not demographically neutral. The ILO’s original 2023 Gmyrek, Berg & Bescond paper found clerical work to be the occupational group with the highest generative-AI exposure, because many clerical tasks are text-, record-, accounting-, scheduling-, and information-processing tasks that can be codified or assisted by LLMs (https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-global-analysis-potential-effects-job-quantity-and). The 2025 ILO-NASK update keeps that conclusion: globally, about 25% of employment is in occupations with some GenAI exposure; high-income countries are at 34%; and “Gradient 4” highest-exposure jobs are 9.6% of female employment in high-income countries versus 3.5% of male employment, roughly a 2.7x ratio (https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure). The mechanism is occupational segregation, not female biological or cognitive difference: the ILO’s 2026 gender brief reports female-dominated occupations are exposed at 29% versus 16% for male-dominated occupations, and 16% of female-dominated occupations fall into the highest exposure categories versus 3% of male-dominated ones (https://www.ilo.org/publications/gen-ai-occupational-segregation-and-gender-equality-world-work).

For the U.S., “clerical” is not an abstraction. BLS CPS 2025 data show office and administrative support occupations employ 16.444 million people and are 70.5% women; they are 14.9% Black, 5.6% Asian, and 20.0% Hispanic or Latino, with Hispanic ethnicity counted separately from race (https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11.htm). Secretaries and administrative assistants except legal, medical, and executive number 1.491 million and are 91.9% women; general office clerks number 1.436 million and are 80.5% women (https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11.htm). Pay is middle-to-lower-middle, not elite: secretaries and administrative assistants had a May 2024 median annual wage of $47,460; the non-executive, non-legal, non-medical subgroup was $46,290; office and administrative support occupations overall were $46,320 (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/secretaries-and-administrative-assistants.htm). BLS full-time weekly earnings for 2025 put office and administrative support at $981 per week, and secretaries/administrative assistants except legal, medical, and executive at $960 overall and $940 for women (https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat39.htm).

EMPIRICAL dispute: exposure is not displacement. The ILO explicitly frames GenAI’s dominant near-term effect as task transformation rather than full job automation, because most occupations combine automatable and nonautomatable tasks (https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/one-four-jobs-risk-being-transformed-genai-new-ilo%E2%80%93nask-global-index-shows). But displacement incidence matters because displaced workers do not all “snap back.” BLS’s January 2024 Displaced Worker Survey found 2.578 million long-tenured workers had been displaced in 2021-2023; only 65.7% were reemployed in January 2024, 16.1% were unemployed, and 18.2% had left the labor force (https://www.bls.gov/news.release/disp.htm). Among long-tenured displaced workers who lost full-time wage-and-salary jobs and were reemployed in January 2024, 62% earned as much or more than on the lost job, meaning roughly 38% earned less (https://www.bls.gov/news.release/disp.nr0.htm). Distribution is uneven over longer horizons: a Brookings PSID study found Black workers were 67% more likely than white workers to experience displacement, workers without a bachelor’s degree were 67% more likely than those with a bachelor’s degree, and annual earnings fell 57% in the year after displacement and remained 25% lower in the tenth year (https://www.brookings.edu/articles/job-displacement-in-the-united-states-by-race-education-and-parental-income/).

That links the AI debate to the corpus’s China-shock material. Autor, Dorn, and Hanson found Chinese import exposure increased unemployment, lowered labor-force participation, reduced wages, and explained about one-quarter of the contemporaneous decline in U.S. manufacturing employment (https://www.nber.org/papers/w18054). Their later persistence work found adverse effects on manufacturing employment, employment-population ratios, and income per capita still visible through 2019, with adjustment occurring less through migration than through exit from work (https://www.nber.org/papers/w29401). The strongest displacement-harm evidence is not merely wage loss but mortality and morbidity: Case and Deaton document rising “deaths of despair” from drugs, suicide, and alcohol among less-educated Americans, with Princeton summarizing that deaths of despair claim more than 150,000 American lives each year and that adults without a degree are “literally dying from pain and despair” (https://deathsofdespair.princeton.edu/about). A related Case-Deaton PNAS study found adult life expectancy from ages 25 to 75 fell after 2010 for Americans without a bachelor’s degree while continuing to improve for those with one (https://www.princeton.edu/news/2021/03/08/life-expectancy-falling-adults-without-bachelors-degree).

The counterpoint is real. Pew’s 2023 occupational exposure analysis found high-AI-exposure jobs are disproportionately higher paid: workers in the most exposed jobs earned $33 per hour on average in 2022 versus $20 in the least exposed jobs; college graduates in the most exposed jobs earned $41 versus $26 in the least exposed college-graduate jobs (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/07/26/earnings-of-workers-with-more-or-less-exposure-to-ai/). Pew also found bachelor’s-degree workers were more than twice as likely as high-school-only workers to be in the highest-exposure jobs, 27% versus 12% (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2023/07/26/which-u-s-workers-are-more-exposed-to-ai-on-their-jobs/). The optimistic EMPIRICAL argument is that this transition burdens workers with savings, networks, credentials, and remote-work options. The rebuttal is that credential value may be the asset being depreciated: if junior analyst, coding, paralegal, bookkeeping, and administrative tasks become cheap, the pathway by which credentials convert into experience also narrows.

**2. Rival Hypothesis: Offshoring and Global Labor Arbitrage**

A serious U.S. forecast must separate automation from geographic reallocation. India’s global capability center buildout is large enough to matter. NASSCOM’s FY2024 GCC landscape says India had over 1,700 GCCs, roughly 2,975 centers, $64.6 billion in revenue, and more than 1.9 million employees; it projected the GCC market near $100 billion and headcount above 2.5 million by 2030 (https://community.nasscom.in/index.php/communities/nasscom-insights/india-gcc-landscape-report-5-year-journey?page=7). The Zinnov-NASSCOM mid-market GCC report adds that India had 1,760+ GCCs in FY2025E, with 480+ mid-market GCCs employing 210,000+ professionals, and 35% of those mid-market centers established in FY2023-FY2025E (https://zinnov.com/centers-of-excellence/zinnov-nasscom-mid-market-global-capability-centers-gccs-2025-report/).

EMPIRICAL: direct public evidence apportioning U.S. junior-role decline between AI and offshoring is thin. UNVERIFIED as a causal split: “U.S. junior role decline is partly GCC reallocation” is plausible but not cleanly identified in public data. The AI-side evidence is now stronger: a 2026 Census CES working paper found early-career workers aged 22-24 in the most AI-exposed industry-state cells had regression-adjusted employment declines of 12% over 10 quarters after ChatGPT, driven by lower hiring (https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html). But Indeed’s 2025 tech-postings analysis cautioned there was no “smoking gun” tying weak tech postings to AI, because nearly half the net decline from the tech-postings peak occurred before ChatGPT and broader economic conditions also mattered (https://www.hiringlab.org/2025/07/30/the-us-tech-hiring-freeze-continues/). The Federal Reserve similarly found no overall reduction in job postings among AI-adopting firms or industries through its 2026 analysis, while warning that pockets of worker harm can coexist with stable aggregate postings (https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/ai-adoption-and-firms-job-posting-behavior-20260327.html).

The Philippines is the cleaner “natural experiment” for AI in offshore voice and back-office labor. Philippine IT-BPM employment rose from 1.7 million FTEs in 2023 to 1.82 million in 2024, with revenue rising to $38 billion (https://www.pna.gov.ph/articles/1241876). IBPAP reported 2025 revenue above $40 billion and headcount around 1.9 million, with baseline 2026 targets of $42 billion and 1.97 million workers (https://www.bworldonline.com/top-stories/2026/01/29/727110/ibpap-cautiously-optimistic-for-it-bpm-sector-in-2026/). The contact-center/business-process segment reportedly grew from 1.62 million workers in 2024 to 1.68 million in 2025, with a 2026 projection of 1.73 million (https://www.philstar.com/the-freeman/cebu-business/2026/06/13/2534925/philippines-bpo-sector-grows-4-168m-despite-ai-fears). Reading one: AI is augmenting call centers and enabling higher-value “AI-assisted CX,” so offshore jobs survive. Reading two: aggregate headcount masks task downgrading, tighter monitoring, and slower future growth once automated call handling matures. Through available 2026 public numbers, however, the Philippine BPO case does not show an employment collapse.

The 15-year U.S. forecast differs sharply by cause. If automation dominates, the U.S. faces economy-wide labor-demand pressure and credential depreciation. If offshoring dominates, U.S. employment loss is concentrated in tradable services, while global employment may rise and U.S. policy levers are trade, procurement, tax, labor standards, and education rather than universal post-work redistribution.

**3. The Labor the Corpus Couldn’t See**

AI already is jobs. Data annotation, RLHF, safety evaluation, and content moderation are not edge cases; they are AI production. Fairwork’s 2025 cloudwork report evaluates 16 global cloudwork platforms, including data annotation services, and frames the sector as involving millions of workers in AI supply chains (https://fair.work/en/fw/publications/cloudwork-report-2025/). Appen says it maintains a global crowd of more than 1 million contributors across 235+ languages (https://www.appen.com/press-release/state-of-ai-2024). A Scale-commissioned Oxford Economics report estimates the U.S. data annotation market alone at $2.7-$5.0 billion in 2024, directly supporting nearly 200,000 flexible earning opportunities and 9,000 additional full-time employee jobs; because Scale commissioned it, treat the market-benefit framing as interested but the figures as usable with caution (https://www.oxfordeconomics.com/resource/the-economic-impact-of-the-data-annotation-industry/).

Conditions are disputed but credible reporting is grim. TIME reported OpenAI’s Kenyan data labelers through Sama were paid take-home wages of about $1.32-$2 per hour while reviewing graphic sexual abuse, violence, suicide, and hate content; TIME also reported OpenAI paid Sama $12.50 per worker-hour (https://time.com/6247678/openai-chatgpt-kenya-workers/). Sama disputes exploitation claims and says it paid above Kenyan living and minimum wages and later exited content moderation (https://www.sama.com/blog/ethical-and-sustainable-sourcing). Equidem’s 2025 investigation interviewed 113 workers across Colombia, Ghana, Kenya, and the Philippines and reported psychological, sexual, occupational, and economic harms among content moderators and data labelers (https://equidem.org/reports/scroll-click-suffer-the-hidden-human-cost-of-content-moderation-and-data-labelling/). Kenya litigation over Meta/Sama moderators shows courts grappling with whether lead platforms can be treated as employers when work is formally outsourced (https://new.kenyalaw.org/akn/ke/judgment/keca/2024/1152/eng%402024-09-20).

The displacement dynamic is visible inside annotation itself. Appen’s own human-in-the-loop discussion reports an entropy-informed LLM annotation process that maintained 87% accuracy while saving up to 62% of annotation costs and reducing time by a factor of 3 (https://www.appen.com/blog/human-in-the-loop-approach-ai-data-quality). In 2025, xAI reportedly laid off 500 generalist data-annotation workers while pivoting toward specialist AI tutors, about one-third of a 1,500-person annotation team (https://techcrunch.com/2025/09/13/xai-reportedly-lays-off-500-workers-from-data-annotation-team). That is the miniature version of the broader forecast: general cognitive labor is compressed; scarce expert labor is bid up.

For many workers, the main AI experience is not replacement but algorithmic management. Dubal defines algorithmic wage discrimination as granular data producing unpredictable, variable, personalized hourly pay, based on ethnographic work with on-demand workers (https://www.columbialawreview.org/content/on-algorithmic-wage-discrimination/). Warehouse management shows the physical version: the Senate HELP Committee’s 2024 Amazon report found Amazon warehouses recorded over 30% more injuries than the warehousing industry average in 2023 and that workers were nearly twice as likely to be injured as other warehouse workers in each of the past seven years (https://www.help.senate.gov/dem/newsroom/press/news-sanders-releases-sweeping-report-exposing-how-amazons-obsession-with-speed-injures-workers-at-unprecedented-rates). OSHA’s 2024 settlement with Amazon required corporate-wide ergonomic measures after multi-site cases involving lower-back and musculoskeletal hazards (https://www.osha.gov/news/newsreleases/osha-national-news-release/20241219). The management steelman is that measurement can improve productivity and safety: Amazon says its global recordable incident rate improved 34% over five years and lost-time incident rate improved 65%, and that robotics reduced repetitive strain (https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/amazon-workplace-safety-performance-2024). EMPIRICAL dispute: whether algorithmic systems are mainly control devices or productivity/safety tools depends on transparency, worker power, and whether gains are shared.

Worker voice is mixed but anxious. Pew’s 2025 worker survey found 52% of U.S. workers were worried about future workplace AI, 36% hopeful, 33% overwhelmed, and 32% expected fewer long-run job opportunities for themselves; only 6% expected more opportunities (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/). Among workers who used AI chatbots for work, 40% said they were extremely or very helpful for speed, but only 29% said the same for improving quality (https://www.pewresearch.org/social-trends/2025/02/25/u-s-workers-are-more-worried-than-hopeful-about-future-ai-use-in-the-workplace/).

**4. Where the Jobs Actually Are and Will Be**

BLS 2024-2034 projections are an antidote to pure white-collar tunnel vision. Total U.S. employment is projected to rise by 5.212 million jobs, from 169.956 million to 175.168 million (https://www.bls.gov/emp/tables/fastest-growing-occupations.htm). Home health and personal care aides are the largest projected-growth occupation: 4.348 million jobs in 2024, 5.088 million in 2034, +739,800 jobs, +17.0%, with a 2024 median wage of $34,900 (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/home-health-aides-and-personal-care-aides.htm). BLS notes this one occupation accounts for roughly one of every seven jobs added over the decade (https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2026/article/industry-and-occupational-employment-projections-overview.htm).

A rough physically anchored, lower-cognitive-AI-exposure basket from BLS occupational groups includes healthcare support (+988,300), construction/extraction (+394,100), installation/maintenance/repair (+301,400), personal care/service (+186,500), transportation/material moving (+579,900), and food preparation/serving (+489,900). That totals about 2.94 million jobs, or 56% of projected net growth; this is my calculation from BLS group projections, not a BLS “AI exposure” category (https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2026/article/industry-and-occupational-employment-projections-overview.htm). The complacent reading is that most job growth remains physically anchored. The doomer reading is that the escape valve pays poorly: home health/personal care aides at $34,900 are $12,560 below the $47,460 median for secretaries/admin assistants and about $39,360 below executive secretaries/admin assistants at $74,260 (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/secretaries-and-administrative-assistants.htm; https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/home-health-aides-and-personal-care-aides.htm). If the displaced worker is a roughly $80,000 executive assistant, the replacement care job is about $16.78/hour on a full-time annualized basis.

Autor’s optimistic alternative is not “learn to code.” His 2024 NBER argument is that AI could extend expert decision support downward, allowing more workers with complementary knowledge to perform higher-stakes tasks now monopolized by doctors, lawyers, software engineers, and professors; he explicitly calls this “not a forecast but an argument about what is possible” (https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140). Critics reply that firms can use the same systems to deskill, monitor, and automate, and that institutional design determines whether AI restores middle-skill work or hollows it out further.

Growth-theory optimism is also coherent. Aghion, Jones, and Jones model AI as potentially automating production of goods and ideas; if AI accelerates idea production, it can raise growth, though bottlenecks and distributional conflict can limit gains (https://www.nber.org/papers/w23928). Acemoglu-Restrepo task models similarly show automation can displace labor in tasks, but productivity effects, capital accumulation, and creation of new labor-intensive tasks can raise labor demand if the reinstatement/productivity effects dominate (https://www.nber.org/papers/w24196). EMPIRICAL dispute: the sign of net labor demand is not a law of nature; it is a race between task displacement, new tasks, demand expansion, and bargaining.

Care work resists automation because it is embodied, relational, mobile, and trust-heavy: BLS describes aides working in clients’ homes, helping people move, dealing with cognitive impairment and behavioral risks, and providing long-term support (https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/home-health-aides-and-personal-care-aides.htm). NORMATIVE dispute: channeling displaced clerical workers into care can be described as useful social reallocation, or as failure if the receiving jobs are low-paid, injury-prone, and low-autonomy.

**Revised Disagreement Propositions**

1. EMPIRICAL: AI exposure will translate into displacement mainly where tasks are both automatable and organizationally separable.  
Resolution condition: occupation-level employment, hiring, wage, and task-content data show whether high-exposure clerical and junior professional roles shrink faster than matched low-exposure roles for at least eight quarters.

2. EMPIRICAL: U.S. junior white-collar decline is partly offshoring/GCC reallocation, not just automation.  
Resolution condition: firm-level data link U.S. entry-level cuts to simultaneous India/Philippines/GCC hiring in the same functions; absent that, this remains UNVERIFIED.

3. EMPIRICAL: AI will be experienced by most workers first as algorithmic management, not unemployment.  
Resolution condition: worker surveys and administrative data show growth in automated scheduling, quota, pay, monitoring, and evaluation systems exceeding AI-attributable layoffs.

4. NORMATIVE: An economy where displaced $75,000-$80,000 administrative workers become $34,900 care workers has “adjusted.”  
Resolution condition: agreement on whether adjustment means any reemployment, comparable earnings, comparable autonomy, or socially useful work with wage insurance.

5. EMPIRICAL/NORMATIVE: AI can rebuild middle-class work only if institutions steer it toward expertise extension rather than labor substitution.  
Resolution condition: measurable growth in middle-wage AI-complemented occupations, shared productivity gains, and portable training ladders rather than rising profits with falling entry-level hiring.
