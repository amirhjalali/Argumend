**Report**

Question: **Should the United States substantially reduce its military and diplomatic support for Israel?**  
Here “substantially reduce” means materially cutting annual military aid, slowing or stopping offensive arms transfers, and/or reducing U.S. diplomatic protection at the UN.

**Material Facts**

The current U.S.-Israel military aid framework is the 2016 ten-year Memorandum of Understanding covering FY2019-FY2028, under which the U.S. pledged, subject to congressional appropriation, **$38 billion**: **$33 billion in Foreign Military Financing** and **$5 billion for missile defense**, normally disbursed as **$3.3 billion FMF plus $500 million missile-defense funding per year**. https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/the-press-office/2016/09/14/fact-sheet-memorandum-understanding-reached-israel The MOU also phases out Israel’s “off-shore procurement” privilege, with OSP ending by FY2028, shifting more spending toward U.S.-based defense production. https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RL33222.html

Since October 7, Congress has added emergency aid above the MOU. CRS says P.L. 118-50, enacted in April 2024, included **$3.5 billion in FMF for Israel** and **$5.2 billion for missile defense**, including **$4 billion for missile-defense systems** and **$1.2 billion for Iron Beam**. https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/RL/RL33222?os=qtfT_2 CFR calculates that enacted U.S. direct military aid to Israel after October 7 reached at least **$16.3 billion** when the April 2024 supplemental and FY2024/FY2025 MOU appropriations are counted. https://www.cfr.org/articles/us-aid-israel-four-charts

Public arms-transfer data are incomplete because many transfers fall below formal notification thresholds or come from existing FMS cases. The Washington Post reported that by March 2024 the Biden administration had approved and delivered **more than 100 separate military sales** to Israel since October 7, including precision-guided munitions, small-diameter bombs, bunker-buster munitions, and small arms; that aggregate claim depends on unnamed officials and is therefore **UNVERIFIED as to full list and value**. https://www.washingtonpost.com/national-security/2024/03/06/us-weapons-israel-gaza/ Publicly announced emergency sales in December 2023 included **nearly 14,000 120mm tank cartridges** worth **$106.5 million** and **155mm artillery ammunition** worth **$147.5 million**. https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/04/02/us-military-aid-israel-gaza-biden/ In May 2024, President Biden acknowledged pausing one shipment that included **2,000-pound and 500-pound bombs** over Rafah concerns while saying defensive systems such as Iron Dome would continue. https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/04/02/us-military-aid-israel-gaza-biden/

Major 2025 public FMS notifications included **$6.75 billion** for munitions, JDAM kits, fuzes, and support, with deliveries estimated to begin in 2025. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4060920/israel-munitions-guidance-kits-fuzes-and-munitions-support They also included **3,000 AGM-114 Hellfire missiles** for **$660 million**, with deliveries estimated to begin in 2028. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4060949/israel-agm-114-hellfire-missiles They included **$675.7 million** in 1,000-pound bomb bodies and JDAM kits, also with deliveries estimated to begin in 2028. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4088252/israel-munitions-guidance-kits-and-munitions-support They included **$510 million** for JDAM guidance kits on June 30, 2025. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4230375/israel-munitions-guidance-kits-and-munitions-support In 2026, DSCA listed proposed sales to Israel of **$1.98 billion in Joint Light Tactical Vehicles**, **$3.8 billion in AH-64E Apache helicopters**, **$740 million in Namer armored-personnel-carrier power packs**, and **$150 million in AW119Kx helicopters**. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Tag/47509

At the UN Security Council, the U.S. vetoed Gaza-related ceasefire or humanitarian-pause texts on **October 18, 2023**, **December 8, 2023**, **February 20, 2024**, **November 20, 2024**, **June 4, 2025**, and **September 18, 2025**, and also vetoed a Russian amendment on **December 22, 2023**. https://press.un.org/en/2023/sc15450.doc.htm https://www.un.org/unispal/document/us-vetoes-resolution-on-gaza-which-called-for-immediate-humanitarian-ceasefire-dec8-2023/ https://www.un.org/unispal/document/security-council-meeting-coverage-20feb2024/ https://www.un.org/unispal/document-category/vetoed-draft-resolution/ https://www.un.org/unispal/document/ceasefire-in-gaza-security-council-draft-resolution-vetoed-by-the-united-states-s-2025-353/ https://www.un.org/unispal/document/security-council-press-release-18sep25/

**The Strongest Case for Continuing Support**

The pro-support case begins with threat environment. Hamas killed about **1,200 people** and took **251 hostages** on October 7, 2023, according to the U.S. Department of Defense. https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/article/3927710/statement-by-secretary-of-defense-lloyd-j-austin-iii-marking-one-year-since-ham/ A UN Commission of Inquiry found that Hamas and other Palestinian armed groups intentionally targeted civilians, took hostages, and committed war crimes on October 7; Hamas disputes some allegations, especially sexual-violence claims. https://www.un.org/unispal/document/coi-report-a-hrc-56-26-27may24/ The UN special representative on sexual violence in conflict found “reasonable grounds” to believe rape and gang rape occurred at several October 7 locations and “clear and convincing information” that hostages were subjected to sexual violence; she also stated some widely reported allegations could not be verified. https://www.un.org/unispal/document/following-visit-to-israel-and-the-occupied-west-bank-un-special-representative-of-the-secretary-general-on-sexual-violence-in-conflict-ms-pramila-patten-finds-sexual-violence-occurred-on-7-october/ CRS describes Hamas as a U.S.-designated foreign terrorist organization that has controlled Gaza since 2007, receives support from Iran and Hezbollah, and has used Gaza to launch attacks on Israel. https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12549

Strategically, supporters argue that Israel is the most militarily capable and politically reliable U.S. partner in a region where Iran, Hezbollah, Hamas, Palestinian Islamic Jihad, and the Houthis pose persistent threats. CRS states that Hamas receives material assistance and training from Iran and allies including Hezbollah. https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/IF12549 The National Counterterrorism Center describes Hamas as the largest and most capable militant group in the West Bank and Gaza and says Israeli operations degraded its Gaza military capabilities after October 7. https://www.odni.gov/nctc/terrorist_groups/hamas.html

Supporters also argue that U.S.-Israel missile-defense cooperation is not charity but joint capability development. The MOU funds Iron Dome, David’s Sling, Arrow, and related systems. https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/RL/RL33222?os=qtfT_2 The Israel Ministry of Defense states that Iron Dome is produced in collaboration with the U.S. government and that the U.S. Missile Defense Agency is a partner in Iron Dome components and in Arrow and David’s Sling co-development and co-production. https://mod.gov.il/en/press-releases/press-room/israel-mod-signs-first-defense-procurement-contract-from-us-aid-package-with-rafael-to-expand-the-serial-production-of-iron-dome-interceptors David’s Sling is jointly developed by Israel’s missile-defense organization and the U.S. Missile Defense Agency, with Rafael as prime contractor and Raytheon as subcontractor. https://mod.gov.il/en/press-releases/press-room/davids-sling-air-and-missile-defense-system-successfully-completes-advanced-upgrade-tests

The pro-support case also emphasizes regional architecture. The U.S. has promoted the Abraham Accords, under which Israel normalized relations with the UAE, Bahrain, Morocco, and Sudan. https://2017-2021.state.gov/u-s-relations-with-israel/ Supporters argue that reducing U.S. backing would weaken the U.S.-led regional camp just as Saudi normalization remains conditional and Iran-aligned groups remain active. CRS says Saudi Arabia has tied normalization to ending Israeli operations in Gaza and advancing Palestinian statehood. https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48162

Finally, supporters point to U.S. industrial benefits. CRS says missile-defense procurement and co-production under the MOU require substantial U.S. spending, and at least **50%** of annual procurement/co-production funds must be spent in the United States. https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/RL/RL33222?os=qtfT_2 Major 2025 and 2026 FMS notices identify U.S. contractors including Boeing, Lockheed Martin, AM General, and U.S. production facilities. https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4060920/israel-munitions-guidance-kits-fuzes-and-munitions-support https://www.dsca.mil/Press-Media/Major-Arms-Sales/Article-Display/Article/4394563/israel-joint-light-tactical-vehicle

**The Strongest Case for Reducing Support**

The reduction case begins with the argument that U.S. support has become strategically costly. U.S. vetoes have repeatedly isolated Washington in the Security Council, including **14-1** votes on June 4, 2025 and September 18, 2025. https://www.un.org/unispal/document/ceasefire-in-gaza-security-council-draft-resolution-vetoed-by-the-united-states-s-2025-353/ https://www.un.org/unispal/document/security-council-press-release-18sep25/ Critics argue that this damages U.S. credibility when it invokes international law in Ukraine, Sudan, or the South China Sea; that is an inference from the voting pattern, not a finding by the UN.

The second argument is entanglement. Iran launched its first direct attack from Iranian territory against Israel on April 13, 2024, according to CRS, and U.S. forces helped defend Israel. https://www.congress.gov/crs-products/product/pdf/RL/RL33222?os=qtfT_2 Critics argue that the more Washington identifies Israel’s campaigns as U.S.-backed, the more U.S. personnel and assets become targets for Iran-aligned escalation; supporters respond that abandonment would invite more aggression.

The third argument is leverage. Critics say unconditional aid has not reliably bought Israeli compliance with U.S. preferences on settlements, humanitarian access, Rafah, or postwar Gaza governance. Biden’s 2024 bomb pause showed that the U.S. can exert tactical pressure, but critics say it did not produce a durable strategic shift. https://www.washingtonpost.com/world/2024/04/02/us-military-aid-israel-gaza-biden/ Supporters counter that aid preserves private access and that visible ruptures would reduce U.S. influence rather than increase it.

The fourth argument is opportunity cost. Israel is a high-income state with a large domestic defense sector, while U.S. FMF and supplemental funds consume scarce attention, weapons, interceptors, and diplomatic capital. CFR reports that Israel has received more U.S. aid than any country since World War II. https://www.cfr.org/articles/us-aid-israel-four-charts Supporters answer that most FMF is spent on U.S. systems and that Israel’s capabilities reduce the need for larger U.S. deployments.

The fifth argument is Arab and Muslim world relations. Saudi Arabia has publicly conditioned normalization on Palestinian statehood and an end to the Gaza war. https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48162 Critics argue that visible U.S. backing for Israel’s Gaza campaign undercuts normalization, counter-extremism, and U.S. soft power; supporters argue that Gulf governments still share U.S. and Israeli concerns about Iran and may resume normalization once Gaza stabilizes.

**Legal and Normative Dispute**

The legal dispute is not settled by slogans. The ICJ’s January 26, 2024 provisional-measures order found that some rights claimed by South Africa under the Genocide Convention were “plausible,” that at least some alleged acts appeared capable of falling within the Genocide Convention, and that the case could proceed at the provisional stage; it did **not** make a merits finding that Israel committed genocide. https://www.icj-cij.org/node/203447 On March 28 and May 24, 2024, the ICJ ordered additional measures related to humanitarian assistance and Rafah; Israel disputes South Africa’s genocide allegations and argues it is fighting Hamas, not Palestinians as a group. https://www.icj-cij.org/node/204091

The ICJ’s July 19, 2024 advisory opinion found Israel’s continued presence in the occupied Palestinian territory unlawful and called for ending it as rapidly as possible; Israel and the United States rejected or criticized aspects of that opinion, and advisory opinions are not the same as criminal convictions. https://www.icj-cij.org/node/204176

The ICC issued arrest warrants on November 21, 2024 for Benjamin Netanyahu and Yoav Gallant after finding reasonable grounds to believe they bore responsibility for war crimes and crimes against humanity, including starvation as a method of warfare; this is a charging-stage standard, not a conviction. https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-state-palestine-icc-pre-trial-chamber-i-rejects-state-israels-challenges Israel disputes ICC jurisdiction over Israeli nationals, while the ICC chamber said Israel’s acceptance was not required because the court relied on Palestine’s territorial jurisdiction. https://www.icc-cpi.int/news/situation-state-palestine-icc-pre-trial-chamber-i-rejects-state-israels-challenges President Biden called the warrants “outrageous” in November 2024. https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/DCPD-202401020 The Trump administration later sanctioned ICC officials, and human-rights groups sued over those sanctions in August 2026. https://apnews.com/article/8b71013c8a426582b5e5425d2b99e8e2

On U.S. law, the Leahy Law prohibits assistance to foreign security-force units where the U.S. has credible information of gross human-rights violations unless remediation occurs. https://www.state.gov/leahy-law-fact-sheet/ In April 2024, the State Department said it had reviewed Israeli units and found several pre-October 7 gross-violation cases, with four remediated and one still under review; no broad cutoff followed. https://www.state.gov/briefings/department-press-briefing-april-29-2024/ The Biden administration’s NSM-20 report reportedly found it reasonable to assess that U.S. weapons had been used by Israeli forces in ways inconsistent with international humanitarian law, but did not conclude that Israel’s assurances were not credible enough to require a cutoff; supporters call that a lawful, evidence-bound standard, while critics call it an evasion of U.S. arms law. https://www.foreign.senate.gov/press/dem/release/chair-cardin-on-developments-in-israel-the-administrations-nsm-20-report

**Humanitarian Record and Casualty Disputes**

The Gaza casualty record is heavily disputed, but the range is not unknowable. OCHA reports Gaza Ministry of Health figures while warning that UN verification is incomplete. https://www.ochaopt.org/content/reported-impact-snapshot-gaza-strip-1-july-2026 As of August 27, 2025, OCHA cited Gaza MoH figures of **62,895 Palestinian fatalities** and **158,927 injuries** since October 7, 2023. https://www.un.org/unispal/document/ocha-sitrep-gaza-28aug25/ A January 2026 Jewish Telegraphic Agency report said a senior IDF official estimated the Gaza death toll at about **71,000**, roughly matching the Gaza Health Ministry’s then-current estimate; that report is not itself an official IDF publication. https://www.jta.org/2026/01/29/israel/idf-official-says-71000-killed-in-gaza-war-matching-gaza-health-ministry-estimate OCHA reported **1,180 additional fatalities** and **3,810 injuries** after the October 10, 2025 ceasefire announcement. https://www.ochaopt.org/content/humanitarian-situation-report-23-july-2026

Methodological disputes matter. Gaza MoH counts reported direct deaths and generally does not distinguish combatants from civilians in its top-line totals. https://www.ochaopt.org/content/humanitarian-situation-report-200-gaza-strip Israel has argued that many killed were Hamas combatants; Netanyahu said in May 2024 that roughly **14,000 combatants** and **16,000 civilians** had been killed, but CBS noted that neither Israel nor Hamas had provided evidence sufficient to verify their respective estimates. https://www.cbsnews.com/news/israel-hamas-war-gaza-death-toll-netanyahu-un-civilians-women-children/ ACLED found in 2024 that Israel claimed around **17,000** gunmen killed, while detailed reports it could code accounted for about **8,500 militant fatalities**. https://acleddata.com/report/after-year-war-hamas-militarily-weakened-far-eliminated

Independent public-health work points in different directions. A Lancet capture-recapture analysis estimated traumatic-injury mortality from October 7, 2023 to June 30, 2024 above the MoH identified-death count, but its model depends on assumptions about overlapping casualty lists. https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0140673624026783 A separate Lancet correspondence argued that total direct and indirect deaths attributable to the war could ultimately reach **186,000 or more**, but that was a projection about eventual excess mortality, not a verified current death toll. https://www.thejournal.ie/gaza-death-toll-could-be-close-to-190000-new-lancet-report-6430273-Jul2024/ ABC corrected its own reporting to clarify that the 186,000 figure should not be treated as a current count. https://www.abc.net.au/news/corrections/2024-11-27/gaza-death-estimates/104654654

**Domestic Politics and Lobbying**

U.S. opinion has shifted sharply by party and age. Gallup found approval of Israel’s military action in Gaza fell from **50% in November 2023** to **32% in July 2025**, with **71% of Republicans**, **25% of independents**, and **8% of Democrats** approving in July 2025. https://news.gallup.com/poll/692948/u.s.-back-israel-military-action-gaza-new-low.aspx Gallup found adults **18-34** were already strongly negative in November 2023, with **67% disapproving** of Israel’s military action. https://news.gallup.com/poll/545045/americans-back-israel-military-action-gaza.aspx Pew found in March 2025 that **53%** of U.S. adults viewed Israel unfavorably, including **69% of Democrats** and **37% of Republicans**, and that younger Republicans under 50 were split almost evenly. https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/04/08/how-americans-view-israel-and-the-israel-hamas-war-at-the-start-of-trumps-second-term/ Pew found in September 2025 that **33%** of Americans said the U.S. was providing too much military assistance to Israel, while **8%** said not enough and **35%** were unsure. https://www.pewresearch.org/politics/2025/10/03/how-americans-view-the-israel-hamas-conflict-2-years-into-the-war/

The lobbying debate is real but often overstated in both directions. AIPAC says it lobbies for a strong U.S.-Israel relationship and reported **$3.3 million** in direct lobbying spending in 2024, ranking **191st** by OpenSecrets according to AIPAC’s own page. https://aipac.org/truth-lobbying AIPAC-linked political committees and allied super PACs have also spent heavily in Democratic primaries, which is legally distinct from lobbying but politically central to the influence debate. https://www.theguardian.com/us-news/2026/aug/06/aipac-super-pacs-spend-millions-california J Street describes itself as a pro-Israel, pro-peace advocacy group supporting diplomacy and a two-state outcome. https://jstreet.org/about-us/

Mearsheimer and Walt argued that U.S. support for Israel cannot be explained by strategic or moral grounds alone and is strongly shaped by a loose “Israel lobby.” https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/israel-lobby-and-us-foreign-policy-1 Serious critics, including Brookings’ Walter Russell Mead, argued that their thesis misdescribes U.S. policymaking, underweights public opinion and evangelical politics, and treats a diverse policy coalition as more coordinated than it is. https://www.brookings.edu/articles/testing-the-israel-lobby-thesis/ The fair synthesis is that organized pro-Israel advocacy clearly matters in Congress and primaries, but it operates alongside public opinion, strategic beliefs, party coalitions, defense interests, evangelical politics, donor networks, and presidential choices.

**Regional Order**

A substantial reduction could produce several plausible outcomes. One prediction is deterrence erosion: Iran and its partners may read U.S. distancing as an opportunity to pressure Israel and U.S. regional assets. Another is diplomatic repair: Arab and Muslim publics may view reduced U.S. arms support and fewer vetoes as evidence that Washington is not giving Israel a blank check. A third is limited effect: Israel may self-fund more procurement while U.S. leverage falls. A fourth is conditional normalization: Saudi Arabia and Gulf states may be more willing to engage if reduced U.S. support is tied to a Palestinian political horizon, but CRS reports Saudi Arabia’s public conditions include Palestinian statehood and ending Israeli operations in Gaza. https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48162 None of these predictions is mechanically provable before the policy is tried.

**Core Disagreements**

1. **EMPIRICAL:** Does U.S. military aid materially change Israeli battlefield behavior?  
Evidence that would help: declassified transfer records, Israeli cabinet records, and before/after cases where U.S. pauses changed operational decisions.

2. **EMPIRICAL:** Are Gaza casualty figures closer to MoH/OCHA counts, Israeli combatant-ratio claims, or higher excess-mortality estimates?  
Evidence that would help: independent access to death registries, cemetery records, missing-person lists, rubble-recovery data, and household mortality surveys.

3. **PREDICTIVE:** Would reduced U.S. support deter Israeli excesses or embolden Hamas, Hezbollah, and Iran?  
Evidence that would help: comparable historical cases and intelligence on adversary decision-making; no evidence can resolve it fully in advance.

4. **NORMATIVE:** Should alliance obligations override complicity concerns when an ally faces a terrorist enemy embedded among civilians?  
Evidence cannot resolve this completely; it is a values dispute about responsibility, proportionality, and acceptable risk.

5. **PROCEDURAL:** Should arms transfers to Israel receive the same transparency and congressional-review treatment as transfers to other states?  
Evidence that would help: full notification data and legal analysis of threshold-splitting and emergency authorities.

6. **EMPIRICAL / PROCEDURAL:** Does U.S. diplomatic protection at the UN increase leverage in private, or remove consequences in public?  
Evidence that would help: declassified U.S.-Israel diplomatic cables, timelines of U.S. demands, and measurable Israeli policy responses.

7. **PREDICTIVE / NORMATIVE:** Is Saudi-Israeli normalization more likely with strong U.S. backing for Israel or with U.S. support conditioned on Palestinian statehood steps?  
Evidence that would help: Saudi negotiating documents and later policy outcomes, but the normative question of what price is acceptable remains a values dispute.
