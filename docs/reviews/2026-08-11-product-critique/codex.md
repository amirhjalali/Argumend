## 1. First-Screen Verdict

Bounce. The first screen promises a taxonomy, not an insight: four abstract position cards and metadata before any surprising answer, takeaway, or personal stakes.

## 2. Top 10 Problems Ranked By Shareability Impact

1. **The page never gives the reader a payoff up front.**  
   Offending element: “4 positions · 54 claims · 50 pieces of evidence · 31 open disputes · 5 cruxes”  
   Why it fails: this is database inventory. A stranger wants to know what they will understand after five minutes.  
   Fix: lead with a one-screen “what this map reveals” summary: “The job-loss debate is probably asking the wrong question: unemployment may stay normal while the entry-level career ladder breaks.”

2. **The first section asks readers to learn your schema before they care.**  
   Offending element: “The positions” followed by “Displacement-now,” “Automation-panic redux,” “Wage-collapse, not unemployment,” “Institutional: who decides, and who pays.”  
   Why it fails: these are internal buckets, not reader-native choices. They feel like analyst labels.  
   Fix: present four camps as plain-language answer cards: “Yes, and it’s already starting”; “No, this is another automation panic”; “Not unemployment, but worse jobs”; “Depends who controls deployment.”

3. **The strongest section is written in claim-graph language.**  
   Offending element: “Firms facing improved AI capability respond primarily by reducing hiring and headcount growth rather than by expanding output or redesigning jobs to use the freed-up capacity.”  
   Why it fails: this is probably the central crux, but it reads like a database node.  
   Fix: rewrite cruxes as human questions: “When AI makes workers more productive, do companies hire fewer people or sell more?”

4. **Progressive disclosure exposes the wrong layer first.**  
   Offending element: “Why it’s contested / What would settle it / Who has stakes here / Evidence”  
   Why it fails: useful analyst scaffolding is being shown before the reader has an intuitive map. It makes the page feel like a schema browser.  
   Fix: default each crux to three lines: question, why it matters, current read. Put “why contested,” settlement criteria, and evidence behind secondary expansion.

5. **The page has no answer posture.**  
   Offending element: “Will AI cause mass unemployment in the U.S. within the next 15 years?” followed by four positions instead of a synthesized read.  
   Why it fails: refusing to orient the reader kills shareability. “Look at this” usually means “this clarified something,” not “this lists positions.”  
   Fix: add a “five-minute read” block: “Strict mass unemployment: not visible yet. Concentrated harm: already plausible. The real fight: who pays when productivity gains break career ladders.”

6. **Trust language arrives as bureaucratic self-defense.**  
   Offending element: “computed from the argument structure, not editorial choice” and “Crux rankings are computed from the argument structure and are reproducible.”  
   Why it fails: it centers process legitimacy instead of reader understanding. It also sounds like the product is anxious about being accused of editorial judgment.  
   Fix: move reproducibility/methodology below. Up top say: “We rank cruxes by how many other claims depend on them.”

7. **The most emotional, shareable stakes are buried far below the fold.**  
   Offending element: “An economy in which displaced administrative or clerical workers move into home-health and personal-care jobs paying $30,000-$40,000 less per year has successfully ‘adjusted’ to AI displacement.”  
   Why it fails: this is the human core of the page. It reframes the debate better than the title does.  
   Fix: make this a featured tension near the top: “If AI doesn’t create mass unemployment but pushes office workers into much lower-paid care jobs, did the economy ‘adjust’?”

8. **The evidence layer is unscannable on mobile.**  
   Offending element: “Klarna’s OpenAI-powered assistant handled 2.3 million conversations…” followed immediately by interest notes, scope notes, and challenges.  
   Why it fails: all evidence gets equal visual weight, so nothing feels like the key proof.  
   Fix: for each claim, show one best supporting fact and one best objection. Hide source caveats inside a “source notes” drawer.

9. **Homepage and topic index sell the product as metrics, not insight.**  
   Offending elements: “Balance 47 of 100, weight 69 of 100” and “Well-mapped, genuinely contested.”  
   Why it fails: readers have no reason to care about balance/weight scores before they have seen a payoff. It feels like a catalog of database rows.  
   Fix: use outcome-oriented teasers: “Sports betting: the freedom argument is weaker than the addiction evidence, but prohibition has its own failure mode.”

10. **“Explore the full argument” is a dump, not a path.**  
   Offending element: “Every claim in the map, with its evidence, objections, and status — nothing behind the curtain.”  
   Why it fails: completeness is being treated as the experience. A reader needs route options, not 45 expandable claims.  
   Fix: split into reader paths: “What we know,” “What is still disputed,” “Where values decide,” “Evidence library.”

## 3. The Shareability Question

For someone to send this with “look at this,” the page needs one memorable reframing above the fold: the AI jobs debate is not mainly “mass unemployment or no mass unemployment”; it is whether normal unemployment statistics can hide a broken entry-level ladder, wage downgrading, and a fight over who pays.

The single most shareable thing buried right now is this idea: “An economy in which displaced administrative or clerical workers move into home-health and personal-care jobs paying $30,000-$40,000 less per year has successfully ‘adjusted’ to AI displacement.” That is the argument people would argue about.

## 5. What To Cut

Cut or hide the top-line inventory: “54 claims,” “50 pieces of evidence,” “31 open disputes.” It advertises workload.

Hide “Held by” blurbs on first view. They are source taxonomy, not reader orientation.

Cut visible “Thin evidence,” “Hidden assumption,” “Status basis,” and “Who has stakes here” from the default view. Keep them as inspection tools.

Hide most interest notes and scope notes until source expansion. They are important, but currently they interrupt comprehension.

Cut “Balance /100” and “Weight /100” from homepage and topic cards unless the page first explains why those scores should matter.

Move methodology footer behind a compact “How this was made” link.

Cut the full claim list as a default reading experience. Keep it as an evidence library after the guided narrative.
